Two months earlier, in the last days of August, Tripoli fell. Gaddafi fled to Sirte, his hometown.
There, according to his security chief, Mansour Dhao, he moved to a new house every four or five days. There was no communication, Dhao said, no television, nothing.
He spent most of his time reading the Koran and praying. And he was very afraid of NATO.
By October, fighters from Misrata and Benghazi had pushed his last loyalists into one district of the city.
Two days before the end, in Tripoli, the US Secretary of State said she hoped he could be captured or killed soon.
His son Mutassim had decided: they had to leave.

The plan was to break out at about half past three in the morning, in the dark.
But loading the wounded took longer than expected. Some were taken from the hospital with their bandages still on.
Eight in the morning. The convoy finally leaves, in daylight. About fifty vehicles, most of them pickup trucks loaded with ammunition.
Gaddafi rides in a Toyota Land Cruiser, with Dhao, a relative and his driver. They try to break out to the west.

About eight thirty. NATO aircraft strike the convoy. In Washington, it's two thirty in the morning.
A missile hits the car in front of his. The airbags in his car go off.
Whose aircraft? NATO never said. That day, American officials, speaking anonymously, said one of them was a US Predator drone.
Six months earlier, Washington had approved armed Predators over Libya.
NATO said later that at the time of the strike, it did not know Gaddafi was in the convoy.

Gaddafi and his men switch cars and take cover in a house.
About twenty vehicles race on to the south. NATO aircraft hit them a second time. NATO says it struck eleven vehicles in all.
Mutassim takes a handful of fighters to look for more cars. According to a survivor, he said: I will try and find you a way out of here.
He never came back.

Now, the crown. Sirte is where, in nineteen ninety-nine, Africa's leaders decided to create the African Union, inspired, they wrote, by his proposals.
In August two thousand eight, more than two hundred African kings and traditional rulers named him king of kings. He asked them for an African military to defend Africa.
In two thousand nine, he became chairman of the African Union. At the United Nations, he spoke in the name of one thousand traditional African kingdoms.
In December two thousand ten, he said Africa should have one army of a million soldiers, to confront NATO.
Ten months later, NATO aircraft stopped his convoy.

About eleven in the morning, fighters from Misrata take Muammar Gaddafi alive.

So who killed him? A young fighter later claimed he did. The UN could not verify it.
Libya's interim government said he died in crossfire. The UN could not confirm that either.
It's often said that a NATO bomb, or an American drone, killed him. The record says he survived both strikes, and was taken alive.
No autopsy report has ever been published. The UN asked for it, and never got it.

That afternoon, Libya's interim prime minister told reporters in Tripoli: Muammar Gaddafi has been killed.
His son Mutassim had also been captured alive. By the afternoon, he was dead too.
At two-oh-seven, Washington time, President Obama walked into the Rose Garden. The dark shadow of tyranny, he said, has been lifted.
Near the hotel where Misrata's brigades had their base, dozens of prisoners from the convoy were later found shot.

For three days, the bodies of the father and the son lay on public display in a meat locker in Misrata. The UN later found that the display broke international law.
Before sunrise on Tuesday, October twenty-fifth, they were buried in the desert, in a grave whose location has never been made public.
On October thirty-first, at one minute to midnight, NATO's mission over Libya ended.
In March twenty twelve, the UN said it could not confirm his death as an unlawful killing, and asked for further investigation.
No trial for his killing has ever been reported.

So here's what the record shows.
In two thousand eight, more than two hundred kings called him the king of kings of Africa. In two thousand eleven, NATO stopped his convoy without knowing he was in it. His own guard's grenade wounded him beside a drainage pipe. And the men from Misrata took him alive.
Fifteen years later, no court has said who killed him, and no one has said where he is buried.
