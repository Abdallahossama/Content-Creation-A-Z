Imagine you build the Muslim Brotherhood from nothing. Then one night, you lose everything: shot in a Cairo street, by men in a car rented for a police chief. How?
Saturday, February twelfth, nineteen forty-nine. Cairo. About half past eight in the evening. In New York, it's lunchtime.
A taxi meter is still ticking. Sixteen piastres. On the floor of the cab, ninety-nine prayer beads. Their owner, Hassan al-Banna, forty-two, has just walked back into a building, calling for an ambulance.
Twenty-one years earlier, with six workmen, he founded the Muslim Brotherhood. This week, that name is in a United States Senate race.
By the end of this file, you'll know who fired, whose car the gunmen escaped in, how a number shouted by a boy survived a cover-up, and why a Senate candidate in Michigan is being asked about this group.
##
That Saturday starts at nine in the morning. A go-between visits the Prime Minister, who agrees to meet Banna's men, on Monday.
Why would a prime minister need talks with a schoolteacher? Go back to the beginning.
Mahmudiyya, a small town in the Nile Delta, nineteen-oh-six. His father leads prayers at the mosque, and repairs watches on the side.
At sixteen, the son goes to a teachers' college in Cairo. With his friends, he starts preaching in mosques, and somewhere new: coffee houses.
In nineteen twenty-seven, he's sent to teach in Ismailia, on the Suez Canal. By his own account, he didn't know exactly where it was.
Ismailia is the canal's town. The Suez Canal Company's offices. British bases nearby. And, he wrote, street signs in the language of the occupation.
At night, he preaches in the coffee houses, and watches who listens hardest.
In March nineteen twenty-eight, six of them come to his home: a carpenter, a barber, an ironer, a driver, a gardener and a bicycle repairman.
In his own account, they tell him: we have nothing to offer except our blood, our lives, our faith, and these few coins.
They swear an oath. He gives them a name: we are brothers in the service of Islam. So, we are the Muslim Brothers.
Their first mosque costs nearly two thousand Egyptian pounds. Five hundred come from an unexpected donor: the Suez Canal Company.
##
Back to that Saturday. One in the afternoon. A messenger finds Banna at home, eating lunch at a low table. He has no telephone anymore. The go-between wants to see him at five.
By now, the movement he started with six workmen is enormous.
A newspaper. Scout troops. Fifteen branches in nineteen thirty-three. More than a thousand by nineteen forty-four. Members, by the estimates of the time: hundreds of thousands.
In nineteen forty-one, British intelligence called it the most serious danger to public security in Egypt.
And around nineteen forty, inside the movement, a second organization was born. The Special Apparatus. Secret, and armed. To test a recruit, he could be ordered to buy a revolver with his own money.
In nineteen forty-eight, the Brotherhood sends volunteers to fight in Palestine. At home, the killings start.
March: a judge is shot dead outside his home by two students who are members of the Brotherhood. People close to Banna said he feared the secret wing was slipping out of his control.
November: police stop a jeep in Cairo. Inside: weapons, grenades, plans and codes. The secret wing, on paper.
Wednesday, December eighth, eleven at night. The radio announces that the government has dissolved the Brotherhood. Police arrest everyone at its headquarters, except one man: Banna.
He tells his colleagues: not arresting me is their proof that they mean to kill me.
Twenty days later, Prime Minister Nuqrashi walks into the Interior Ministry. A young man in a police officer's uniform steps up to him, and opens fire. The gunman is a student, and a member of the Brotherhood.
At the Prime Minister's funeral, crowds chant for Banna's death.
In January, a bomb is left at a Cairo courthouse. Banna writes that the men behind it are neither Brothers nor Muslims. And he asks for talks.
##
Five p.m. Banna arrives at the Young Men's Muslim Association on Queen Nazli Street, with his brother-in-law, Abdel Karim Mansour.
According to the family, his father had begged him not to go out unarmed. Banna answered: our lives are in God's hands.
The talks start in a first-floor office. The government wants names, weapons, a secret radio. Banna wants his detained men released first. Nobody moves.
Five-forty, sunset. They pray together, and wait for a minister who never picks up the phone.
Seven forty-five. According to his driver's later confession, Colonel Mahmoud Abdel Magid, head of criminal investigation, gives him an order: wait near the building at eight. If two men in traditional clothes get in, bring them to me, at the Eden Hotel.
Around eight, the go-between gives up and goes home. A taxi is called for Banna.
On the pavement, his brother-in-law would remember, the street was quiet, and dark. What time is it? Eight. It's like the middle of the night. Banna answered: you comment on everything.
Above Cairo, the moon is almost full.
About eight-thirty. Banna gets into the taxi. Two men open fire.

The gunmen run to a car waiting across the street. A boy in the crowd shouts its number: nine, nine, seven, nine. Then he's gone. Nobody ever finds him.
Banna is still on his feet. He walks back into the building, calling for an ambulance.
A friend picks up the phone. On the line, he said later, was an officer of the political police. Told that Banna had been shot, the officer asked one word: Dead?
Eight forty-five. Police reach the taxi. The meter is still running. On the floor, the prayer beads.
At Qasr al-Aini hospital, he's taken into surgery. His family has always said he was left to bleed. No court ever ruled on that.
That night, a newspaper prints the getaway car's number. Police stop the presses. About five thousand copies are already on the street.
Just after midnight, Hassan al-Banna dies.
At one in the morning, police tell his father: the burial is at nine, with no ceremony.
In the morning, the father said, police refused to send men to carry the coffin. So the women of the family carried it, through streets lined with police.
One outsider gets through to the house to pay his respects: Makram Ebeid, a Christian politician.
##
For three years, the case goes nowhere. Then, in July nineteen fifty-two, the army overthrows the King.
The file is reopened. The getaway car, nine nine seven nine, turns out to have been rented by the Interior Ministry, for its chief of criminal investigation.
Monday, August second, nineteen fifty-four. The verdict. The gunman, a police corporal: life. The colonel and his driver: fifteen years each.
The court found the killing was done, quote, for the account of the King and the government. The King was already in exile.
Within thirteen years, all three were free again.
##
And the movement he built? In nineteen fifty-four, after shots are fired at Nasser at a rally, it's banned again. The Brotherhood says that attempt was staged.
In nineteen sixty-six, its best-known thinker, Sayyid Qutb, is hanged.
President Sadat lets its members out of prison in the nineteen-seventies. In two thousand five, its candidates, running as independents, win eighty-eight seats in parliament.
In two thousand twelve, its candidate, Mohamed Morsi, becomes Egypt's president. A year later, the army removes him, and Egypt's government declares the Brotherhood a terrorist organization.
##
Which is why you're hearing this name in twenty twenty-six.
On January thirteenth, Washington designated three branches of the Brotherhood, in Lebanon, Jordan and Egypt, citing support for Hamas, which began as the Brotherhood's wing in Palestine. The Brotherhood said it would fight the move. Egypt welcomed it.
Then the name reached a US Senate race. In Michigan, the Democratic candidate is Abdul El-Sayed. If he wins, he would be the first Muslim in the United States Senate.
His Republican opponent, Mike Rogers, said El-Sayed had pledged his support to the Muslim Brotherhood. Rogers pointed to donations from people and groups tied to American Muslim organizations. PolitiFact called that cherry-picking.
And on October twenty-fifth, at a rally in Michigan, President Trump called El-Sayed a jihadist Muslim Brotherhood member.
El-Sayed's answer: I have never been a member of the Muslim Brotherhood. I never will be. He calls the attacks deflections and deceptions, based on no evidence.
PolitiFact rated the President's claim Pants on Fire. The Associated Press found no evidence that El-Sayed has any affiliation with the Muslim Brotherhood.
Michigan votes on Tuesday, November third.
##
So here's what the record shows.
It began with six workmen and a schoolteacher in a canal town. In twenty years, it had hundreds of thousands of members, and a secret armed wing. A prime minister was shot by one of its members. Seven weeks later, its founder was shot in the street, and the getaway car led to the Interior Ministry.
Seventy-seven years on, the name he chose still divides governments.
