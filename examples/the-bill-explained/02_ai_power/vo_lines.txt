##
Line two. The standby charge.
This is the line almost nobody has heard of, and it's the biggest one.
Thirteen states and Washington D.C. share one giant power grid, called PJM. About sixty-seven million people.
Every year, PJM holds an auction. It pays power plants to be ready to run three years from now, just in case.
You pay for that auction in the supply part of your bill.
For the year that ended in May twenty twenty-five, that auction cost about two point two billion dollars.
Then data centers started lining up.
The next auction cost fourteen point seven billion. The one after that, sixteen point one billion.
The price per megawatt jumped more than nine times in a single auction.
PJM's own independent market monitor looked at why.
It said data centers were responsible for sixty-three percent of that increase. About nine point three billion dollars.
The last three auctions all hit the legal price cap. And the last two still fell short of the grid's reliability target.
##
Here's where that lands.
In June twenty twenty-five, electric bills in New Jersey jumped seventeen to twenty percent.

##
Line three. The wires.
More power has to travel farther, through new lines and new substations.
That's the delivery part of your bill.
In the first half of twenty twenty-six, utilities asked regulators for eighteen point six billion dollars in rate increases, for gas and electric together.
Not all of that is data centers. Storms, wildfires and old equipment are a big part of it.
But in Virginia, home to the biggest cluster of data centers in the world, regulators have now ordered the utility to shift more of these costs onto the data centers themselves.
##
Line four. The fuel.
About forty percent of America's electricity comes from natural gas.
And in twenty twenty-five, gas cost about sixty percent more than the year before.
That has nothing to do with AI. And it's a big reason bills rose almost everywhere, even in states with almost no data centers.
Maine, New Hampshire and Hawaii all saw big price jumps, without a data center boom.
##
Line five. Who should pay.
This is the fight happening right now in state capitals.
Ohio and Virginia now make big data centers sign long contracts and pay for most of the power they reserve, whether they use it or not.
In Oregon, one utility raised rates for data centers by twenty-nine percent, and cut them slightly for homes.
Researchers at Berkeley Lab found that when big new customers pay their full share, more demand can actually spread the grid's fixed costs and lower prices for everyone.
So the question isn't only how much power AI uses. It's who gets the bill.
