In May nineteen forty-six, the papers said only two men had ever gotten off the Rock. Nobody ever found them.
Bernard Coy, forty-six, a Kentucky bank robber, swept the cellhouse floors. He had a good view of the gun gallery.
In nineteen thirty-seven, a judge had asked him: do you get a thrill out of criminal activities? He said: No, I'm just hardened to it.
Joseph Kretzer and Sam Shockley had already tried to break out together, in nineteen forty-one. They took officers hostage in a workshop and tried to cut through the bars. They failed.
Marvin Hubbard, a kidnapper. Miran Thompson, with a record of eight escapes. And Clarence Carnes, nineteen, the youngest of the six.
##
That week, Thompson asked to be excused from work on Thursday afternoon.
At about one, he told the prison doctor he was sick all over. He got the afternoon off.
At one thirty, one of the two officers on the cellhouse floor walked out.
Above the cells ran the west gun gallery, a steel cage for one armed officer, Bert Burch. Officers could only get into it through armored doors from outside the cell blocks.
Around two, Burch stepped through a door to watch the next block.
Coy climbed the bars. He had a tool made from stolen toilet parts. It pushed two bars from five inches apart to seven.
When Burch came back, Coy hit him. Now he had a rifle and a pistol. They were the only two guns the convicts would ever have.
With the guard's keys, he let Kretzer out of D Block.
##
At two fifteen, a kitchen supervisor walked in and saw an officer's trousers on the floor, and Coy up on the gallery, waving.
Within twenty minutes, nine officers were locked inside two cells: four oh three and four oh two.
Now they needed one more thing. The door to the yard. Key number one oh seven.
Coy demanded to know which key opened it. Officer William Miller told him: one oh seven.
He didn't tell him where it was. The key had been hidden. The convicts never found it.
Then the prison siren went off.
##
At about three, outside cell four oh three, the captain of the guards told Kretzer to keep his men from going outside. If they do, they will get hurt.
Kretzer's answer, as the court later recorded it: you mean they will get killed, don't you?
Then he fired into the two cells. Almost all the officers were hit. William Miller would die the next day.
Miller's own sworn statement, dictated by telephone, begins: On this date, May second, nineteen forty-six, at about three in the afternoon.
##
At three twenty-three, a telegram from the warden reached the United Press. It began: Serious trouble.
It said a convict had a machine gun. He didn't. He had a rifle.
Seven minutes later, boats had circled the island.
At four fifteen, the warden phoned the San Francisco police chief. The rioters, he said, were cornered, but not subdued.
By evening, U.S. Marines from Treasure Island were on the Rock.
At six thirty, a reporter in a police boat watched a hundred and fifty prisoners marched out of the shops, in single file, up the steep stairs to the yard.
Officer Harold Stites, the man who had stopped an escape from this island in nineteen thirty-eight, went into the gun gallery to take it back. He was shot and killed.
At seven oh one in the evening, the sun went down. There was almost no moon. The island was lit by searchlights and flares.
At nine, seven hours in, the fighting was still going.
Late that night, prison officers carried the hostages out, two men at a time.
One of them, Officer Robert Baker, had lain still for hours, pretending to be dead.
Coy, Kretzer and Hubbard held the utility corridor behind the cells of C Block. The other three went back to their cells.
##
Friday morning, William Miller died.
The papers reported mortars. The Marine who did the firing described something else.
A Marine warrant officer, Charles Buckner, climbed onto the cellhouse roof, drilled holes, and lowered grenades inside. About a hundred and fifty, by the Marine major's count.
He fired rifle grenades through the windows until noon. He said later he only quit because his shoulder and face were so sore.
The explosives came from an Army arsenal, sent on the authority of General Joseph Stilwell.
Thousands watched from the hills and the docks. Reporters watched from boats in the bay. On the Aquatic Park pier, a man charged ten cents a look through his telescope.
That afternoon, the firing stopped for hours. Then a convict phoned the warden to ask for a deal.
Warden James Johnston, seventy-one, answered: We'll make a deal when you throw out your guns and ammunition.
Silence.
##
Saturday morning, guards went into the utility corridor behind cell block C.
They found Coy, Kretzer and Hubbard dead, the rifle and the pistol beside them.
At twelve ten in the afternoon, the warden's bulletin said: It seems quite certain that no prisoner escaped.
Late that day, he walked reporters through the battered cellhouse. From the cells, convicts shouted: Hey, Saltwater Johnston.
Two guns. Nine officers in two cells. One key they never had. Not one of them ever reached the yard.
##
Two men who fired no shot, Sam Shockley and Miran Thompson, were tried for murder. The court found they had urged Kretzer on.
The trial took a month. The transcript ran to nearly twenty-two hundred pages.
A jury of six men and six women found all three guilty, and spared the youngest, Clarence Karns, nineteen.
On Friday, December third, nineteen forty-eight, Shockley and Thompson were executed at San Quentin.
A coroner's jury had already called the officers' deaths murder, and the three convicts' deaths justifiable homicide.
Officers Miller and Stites are on the National Law Enforcement Officers Memorial. Stites left a widow and four children. Miller, a widow and two.
##
And Clarence Karns? He was reportedly still on Alcatraz sixteen years later.
