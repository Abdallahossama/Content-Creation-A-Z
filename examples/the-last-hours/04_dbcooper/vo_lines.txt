Wednesday, November twenty-fourth, nineteen seventy-one. Ten minutes past eight at night.
A Boeing seven twenty-seven is flying south over Washington State, ten thousand feet up, in the rain.
Its landing gear is down. The stairs under its tail are hanging open.
In the cockpit, a needle jumps. The crew feels the plane move.
Behind a closed curtain, there should be one passenger. A man who told them he has a bomb.
Five minutes earlier, they asked him if everything was OK. He said it was.
Nobody will ever hear from him again.
You've heard the name D.B. Cooper. It wasn't his name.
The FBI looked at more than eight hundred suspects. It never found out who he was.
This is what its own files say happened that night, hour by hour.

Portland, Oregon. The afternoon before Thanksgiving.
At the Northwest Orient counter, a man in a suit and a raincoat buys a one-way ticket to Seattle.
Eighteen dollars and fifty-two cents, plus a dollar forty-eight in tax. Twenty dollars, cash.
The name on the ticket is Dan Cooper.
He is one of the last to board Flight three-oh-five. He takes seat eighteen E, in the last row.
He carries a dark briefcase. He orders a bourbon and soda.
The flight to Seattle normally takes thirty-six minutes.
##
Two fifty-eight in the afternoon. The wheels leave the runway.
Around the same moment, he hands a flight attendant a note.
She later told the FBI it said something like this. Miss, I am hijacking this plane. I have a bomb. Sit next to me.
She sits down beside him. He opens the briefcase just enough for her to see inside.
Red cylinders. She counted about eight. Wires. A large battery.
He tells her the sticks are dynamite. Nobody would ever find out if they were real.
Then he gives her his demands.
Two hundred thousand dollars. Four parachutes, two for the back and two for the chest. A fuel truck waiting in Seattle.
All of it by five o'clock.
Later, he asks for his note back. Nobody has seen it since.
##
Three twenty. The plane does not land in Seattle. It starts to circle.
The passengers are told there is a minor mechanical problem.
Three twenty-two. A phone rings at the FBI's Seattle office.
A man on Flight three-oh-five has a bomb. He wants two hundred thousand dollars by five. And he wants no FBI agents near the plane.
Up in the air, he is calm. He smokes. He talks.
They are not going to Cuba, he tells her.
She asks him why he picked Northwest. He laughs.
It's not because I have a grudge against your airlines, he says. It's just because I have a grudge.
He looks out the window and says, we're over Tacoma now.
When he hears the parachutes are coming from McChord Air Force Base, he says McChord is only twenty minutes away. It shouldn't take that long.
He seems to know the area.
##
Five fifteen. The deadline has passed.
He tells her it is five fifteen, and he is still waiting.
On the ground, a bank has counted out ten thousand twenty-dollar bills.
Before the money leaves, the bank photographs every single serial number.
The bag weighs nineteen pounds.
##
At about a quarter to six, Flight three-oh-five finally lands at Seattle-Tacoma.
He has it park far from the terminal, on a dark stretch of the airfield.
One car. The stair truck. The fuel truck. No flashing lights.
The flight attendant walks down the stairs and drags the money bag back up.
He looks inside. It looks okay, he says.
Then he lets the thirty-six passengers go.
At one point he offers her a packet of the cash as a tip. She hands it back.
Then the parachutes come aboard.
She offers him a printed sheet on how to jump. He says he doesn't need it.
He cuts open one of the chest parachutes, pulls out its nylon cord, and ties the money bag shut.
Then he gives his flight plan, and she writes it down.
Mexico City. Landing gear down. Flaps at fifteen degrees. Stay under ten thousand feet. Cabin lights out. Nobody behind the first class curtain.
The crew explains they cannot reach Mexico with the gear down. They agree to stop for fuel in Reno, Nevada.
The refueling drags on. He gets nervous. Let's get the show on the road, he says.
Meanwhile, the airline calls Boeing. Can a seven twenty-seven fly with its rear stairs down?
Boeing says yes. It has dropped heavy boxes out of that door in flight.
The other two flight attendants are allowed to leave. One stays with him.

Seven thirty-six in the evening. The plane takes off from Seattle, heading south.
Two planes from McChord follow it into the dark.
About four minutes after takeoff, he stands up. He tells the flight attendant to go to the cockpit, and close the curtain behind her.
The last time she sees him, he has a nylon cord tied around his waist.
She begs him to take the bomb with him. He says he will take it, or disarm it.
Then she goes forward. From now on, nobody can see him.
Seven forty-two. He calls the cockpit. He is having trouble getting the stairs down.
Seven forty-three. A warning light comes on. The rear stairs are open.
The plane is flying south along an airway called Victor twenty-three.
##
Eight oh five. The crew calls him one more time. Is everything OK?
Everything is OK, he says.
Those are the last words anyone recorded from him.
About eight ten. Ten thousand feet. Close to two hundred miles an hour. Nineteen degrees outside, and raining.
The crew feels the plane move. A needle jumps.
Ten days later, the FBI called it a virtual certainty. That was the moment he left the plane.
Down the back stairs, into the dark, with a parachute and nineteen pounds of cash.
Nobody saw him go.
##
But nobody in the cockpit knows that.
For almost three more hours, they fly on toward Reno, with the stairs hanging open and the curtain closed.
The airline even suggests making the cabin colder and climbing a little higher, to leave the man in the back short of oxygen.
Over Oregon, ice starts to form on the plane.
No answer from the back.
Near Reno, the flight attendant makes one last call. Sir, we are going to land now. Please put up the stairs.
Silence.
At about eleven o'clock, the plane lands at Reno with the stairs still down.
She switches on the cabin lights.
There is no one there.
##
Eleven twenty-five. FBI agents climb aboard.
On seat eighteen E, they find a black clip-on tie from J.C. Penney, with a pearl tie clip.
One chest parachute, cut open. One back parachute, never used.
The briefcase is gone. The money is gone. Two parachutes are gone.
And so is Dan Cooper.
##
Within days, the whole country knows the story. But with the wrong name.
A news report turned Dan Cooper into D.B. Cooper. It stuck.
The day after the hijacking, a roll of microfilm with ten thousand serial numbers flies to Washington on an ordinary passenger flight.
The FBI calls the case Norjak. Short for Northwest hijacking.
The next spring, soldiers and agents search the forests of southwest Washington, near a small town called Ariel.
They find nothing. No parachute. No money. No body.
And other men copy him. In one six-month stretch, there are seventeen more attempts to extort money from American airlines.
In April nineteen seventy-two, a man named Richard McCoy hijacks a United flight, takes half a million dollars, and parachutes over Utah. He is caught two days later.
The FBI looked hard at McCoy. It ruled him out. He did not match the flight attendants' descriptions.
On November twenty-fourth, nineteen seventy-two, one year to the day, the government signs a new rule. Rear stairs on passenger jets must not open in flight.
On the seven twenty-seven, the fix is a small metal flap. People call it the Cooper vane.
And from January nineteen seventy-three, every airline passenger in America is screened before boarding. Before that, it was about one in a hundred.
##
Then, for years, nothing.
February nineteen eighty. A family is having a picnic on a sandy beach on the Columbia River, near Vancouver, Washington.
An eight-year-old boy named Brian Ingram finds bundles of rotting twenty-dollar bills in the sand.
The serial numbers match the ransom.
Five thousand eight hundred dollars. Two hundred and ninety bills.
It is the only part of the money that has ever been found.
Years later, scientists found tiny algae on one of the bills. They suggest the money went into the water in summer. Months after a jump in November.
Nobody can explain how it got there.
##
Over the years, the FBI looked at more than eight hundred people.
A former paratrooper. A man whose widow said he confessed. A man named by his niece.
The FBI ruled them out, or found no link.
In two thousand one, it took DNA from the tie. But the sample was weak, and mixed.
In July twenty sixteen, after forty-five years, the FBI stopped actively investigating.
It still asks for one thing. If you find a parachute, or the money, call.
##
And the file still isn't finished.
The FBI keeps putting more of its old pages online.
In February, one of them turned out to be about a pilot from Maine. On a page from nineteen seventy-two, someone had written two words by hand. Eliminate Russell.
A YouTuber and Richard McCoy's children say they found McCoy's parachute rig on the family's property. The FBI took it. According to the family, it gave it back last December, without saying what it found.
In May, a scientific study of the tie came out. It ruled out one of the clues a popular theory depended on.
Last October, the co-pilot of Flight three-oh-five died. He was eighty-six.
And in October, a scrap of one of the ransom bills sold at auction for a hundred and thirty-four thousand dollars.

So here is what the records can tell you, and what they can't.
They can tell you he paid twenty dollars, cash, for seat eighteen E.
That he asked for two hundred thousand dollars and four parachutes, and got them.
That at eight oh five he said everything was OK. And that a few minutes later, the plane moved.
They can't tell you who he was.
They can't tell you if he survived the jump. FBI agents thought he probably didn't.
They can't tell you where he landed, or where the other hundred and ninety-four thousand dollars went.
Or if the bomb was ever real.
At eight oh five, he said everything was OK. That is the last thing anyone can say for certain about Dan Cooper.
