Your phone rings at half past one in the morning. A voice tells you: be at the prime minister's office at three thirty, to carry out an execution. Nobody tells you what will happen in that room.
Saturday, December thirtieth, two thousand six. Baghdad. In New York, it is still Friday. Half past five in the evening.
The man they are going to execute is Saddam Hussein.
For more than three years, American soldiers had kept him alive. In the next few hours, they would hand him over.
By the end of this file, you'll know why it happened so fast, who was in the room, what he carried in his hands, and why two videos of that morning ended up telling two different stories.
##
To understand that morning, go back twenty-four years.
Thursday, July eighth, nineteen eighty-two. A town called Dujail, about thirty-seven miles north of Baghdad. During a visit by Saddam, someone tries to kill him. He survives.
What followed is in the court record. According to the prosecution, hundreds of people from Dujail were detained. Orchards were bulldozed.
In May nineteen eighty-four, Saddam signed a referral sending one hundred and forty-eight men and boys from Dujail to the Revolutionary Court.
On June fourteenth, the court sentenced all of them to death. Two days later, Saddam signed decree number seven seven eight, approving the sentences.
Human Rights Watch later found something worse in the trial documents. According to a nineteen eighty-seven memo, forty-six of those men had already died in custody before they were even sent to the court.
Remember that decree. Twenty-two years later, it came back.
##
Saturday, December thirteenth, two thousand three. American soldiers pulled Saddam Hussein out of a hole on a farm near Tikrit.
He was held by the Americans at Camp Cropper, a detention center near Baghdad's airport.
In October two thousand five, he went on trial before the Iraqi High Tribunal. The first case they chose was Dujail.
During the trial, according to the American ambassador, three defense lawyers were killed.
Sunday, November fifth, two thousand six. The verdict. Crimes against humanity. Death by hanging. Two of his co-defendants received the same sentence.
##
Tuesday, December twenty-sixth. The appeals court upholds the sentence.
The head of the appeals chamber is asked when it will happen. His answer: it cannot exceed thirty days.
It took four.
On Wednesday, a farewell letter, which his lawyer said Saddam had written, appeared online. In it, he told Iraqis not to hate the people of the countries that had attacked them.
On Thursday, two of his half-brothers visited his cell. He gave them a copy of his will.
##
Friday, December twenty-ninth. The Iraqi government wants it done. The Americans still have him.
According to the Defense Department, Iraq held legal custody. But American forces held physical custody, at the Iraqi government's request.
The Americans wanted paperwork. Iraq's president, Jalal Talabani, opposed the death penalty. He would not sign a decree, but according to an Iraqi official, he said he had no objections.
In Washington that afternoon, a federal judge was asked to stop it. She refused. An American court, she ruled, had no power over it.
That evening, according to a US diplomatic cable later published by WikiLeaks, Iraq's national security adviser told American officials that the Iraqi government accepted the responsibility.
The Americans would transport Saddam to the site, and hand him over.
At a dinner in the prime minister's office, according to one Iraqi who was there, someone asked the Americans: who is going to execute him, anyway, you or us?
A few minutes before midnight, Prime Minister Nouri al-Maliki signed a letter to his justice minister. Carry out the hanging, it said.
##
Half past one in the morning. Fourteen phones ring across Baghdad.
One of them belongs to a judge, Munir Haddad. The voice on the line tells him: come to the prime minister's office at three thirty, to carry out the execution.
They gather in the dark at a helicopter landing zone inside the Green Zone. The Americans called it LZ Washington.
There, according to the cable, American marshals searched every one of them and took their phones. For operational security.
Two American helicopters. Seven men in each.
Every phone had been taken. That should have been the end of it.
##
At Camp Cropper, near the airport, Saddam Hussein is woken up.
He dresses carefully. A long black coat over a white shirt. A wool cap. A scarf.
American soldiers take him to a Black Hawk helicopter. The flight is about ten miles.
It lands at Kadhimiya, in northern Baghdad, at the old headquarters of his regime's military intelligence. The Americans now call the base Camp Justice.
About an hour before dawn, an S U V arrives at the gate with six people the Iraqi government called its video personnel and bodyguards. American soldiers search the vehicle and let it through.
Half past five in the morning. According to the New York Times, Iraq takes full custody of Saddam Hussein.
After one thousand one hundred and thirteen days, the Americans step back. No American would be inside.
##
The judge, the prosecutor and an acting minister wait in a small, bare room. A desk. A few chairs. A refrigerator.
Ten minutes later, Saddam walks in, his hands bound in front of him.
Judge Haddad later said he seemed normal, not confused, not afraid.
The deputy prosecutor, Munqith al-Faroon, remembered it differently. He told the American ambassador that Saddam's hands were shaking.
The judge reads the verdict, and the appeal ruling. Saddam shouts over him. Long live the nation, long live the people.
My voice was higher than his, the judge said later.

Six in the morning. He is led down a corridor lined with the witnesses.
The national security adviser asks him if he is afraid.
According to Judge Haddad, Saddam said: I am not afraid. I have chosen this path.
In his hands he carries a dark green Koran. He asks who will make sure it reaches the son of Awad al-Bandar.
Awad al-Bandar was the judge who had sentenced the men of Dujail to death in nineteen eighty-four. Now he was waiting for his own execution.
The deputy prosecutor took the Koran. What if I don't see him, he asked. Keep it, Saddam said, until you meet any of my family.
##
Then something changed in the room.
According to the cable, a guard told Saddam to go to hell. The prosecutor raised his voice. Nobody, he said, was to speak to the prisoner.
And as he turned around, he saw two government officials openly taking pictures with their mobile phones.
The fourteen witnesses had handed over their phones. Somebody else had not.
##
He refused the hood.
As he began to pray, a voice in the room called out a name. Muqtada. The name of the Shiite cleric Muqtada al-Sadr.
On the phone video, Saddam can be heard answering. According to the New York Times, he asked: is this how real men behave?
The deputy prosecutor shouted at them to stop. The man is facing execution, he said.
Saddam began the prayer again. He did not finish it.
At about ten past six in the morning, the sentence was carried out.
In New York, it was ten past ten on Friday night. In Texas, President Bush was asleep.
Sunrise was still an hour away. With it began Eid al-Adha, the holiday of sacrifice, for Iraq's Sunnis.
##
Two videos came out of that room.
The first was the government's. It had no sound, and it stopped before the end.
The second was shot on a phone. By late Saturday, it was on the internet, with every word, every chant.
Iraq's government arrested a guard. Within days, the US military's spokesman, General William Caldwell, told reporters: this is a sovereign nation. But we, as a coalition force, would have done it differently.
President Bush said he wished it had gone on in a more dignified way.
In mid-January, he went further. It looked like it was kind of a revenge killing, he said.
##
That night, the coffin went north.
A plain pine coffin, strapped into an American Black Hawk, one hundred and ten miles to a base near Tikrit. Then an Iraqi convoy to Awja, the village where he was born.
Before dawn on Sunday, Saddam Hussein was buried inside a hall he had built for his hometown.
Two weeks later, his half-brother and Awad al-Bandar were executed too.
Years later, the tomb was destroyed in fighting. Today, no one is sure where his body lies.
##
So what does the record keep?
A decree signed in nineteen eighty-four. One hundred and forty-eight names.
A signature a few minutes before midnight. Fourteen phones ringing at half past one. Fourteen phones taken at a landing pad.
A small room with a refrigerator. A green Koran given to the son of the judge who sentenced Dujail.
And two videos. One that went quiet, and one that didn't.
The court said it could not take more than thirty days. It took four.
