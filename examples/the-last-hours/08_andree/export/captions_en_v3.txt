Imagine you vanish over the Arctic in a balloon. Thirty-three years pass before anyone develops your film. But to this day, nobody can prove how you died.
Wednesday, July fourteenth, eighteen ninety-seven. Seven nineteen in the morning, Greenwich time.
Three Swedes jump out of a balloon basket onto the drifting sea ice, eighty-two degrees north.
One of them, Nils Strindberg, twenty-four, raises his camera and photographs the balloon that was supposed to carry them over the North Pole.
Then he puts the film away. Nobody will see it until nineteen thirty.
They had food, fuel, matches and guns. Within weeks of reaching land, all three were dead.
By the end, you'll have read their last written words, seen what survived on that film, and heard why nobody can prove how they died.
Sunday, July eleventh, eighteen ninety-seven. Three in the morning. Danes Island, in the Arctic, about seven hundred miles from the North Pole.
The harbor has been flat as a mirror. Now the first ripples come in from the south.
It's the wind these men have waited two summers for.
The plan: fill a silk balloon with hydrogen, ride the wind over the North Pole, and come down in Siberia, Alaska or Canada.
It's the idea of Salomon August Andrée, forty-two, an engineer at the Swedish patent office. Alfred Nobel paid about half the bill.
The balloon is called the Eagle. Under it hang three long ropes, meant to drag on the ice and help them steer.
Remember those ropes.
Around eight, Andrée asks for an hour to think. On the roof of the balloon house, he asks the others: Shall we try or not?
Knut Frænkel, twenty-seven, an engineer, says go. Strindberg says: I think we ought to try.
Andrée says nothing.
Back on board, he tells the ship's captain that he has no fully valid reasons against it. So he'll agree. Although with a certain hesitation.
One forty-three in the afternoon, Greenwich time. The three men climb onto the roof of the basket.
One forty-six. Andrée shouts: Cut everywhere!
Three knives cut three lines. The Eagle rises, and the men shout: Long live old Sweden!
Strindberg writes: A strange feeling. Wonderful. Indescribable.
Then the balloon sinks, and the basket dips into the harbor. Over go nine bags of sand.
And a sailor on the shore shouts: The drag ropes are lying on the beach!
The ropes were screwed together in sections, and the joints came apart. Two-thirds of all three ropes stayed behind.
From now on, they go wherever the wind goes. Strindberg's notebook: The guide rope was lost.
Two fourteen. Over a small island called Vogelsang, Strindberg drops a little tin. Inside are farewell words to his fiancée.
Remember the tin.
Then the balloon slides into a cloud, and the people on the shore never see it again.
Five thirty-six. Over the sea ice, they let go of the first four of their thirty-six carrier pigeons.
How many messages from those birds ever reached home? We'll get to that.
Ten at night, under the midnight sun, a message buoy goes over the side: Our journey has so far gone well. Weather lovely. Spirits excellent.
Just after midnight, a cloud blocks the sun. Without its warmth, the Eagle sinks. For the next fifty-five hours, it drags and bumps along the ice.
Monday, July twelfth. The wind swings around. The balloon starts drifting west, back the way it came.
By the afternoon, the basket is hitting the ice eight times in half an hour.
Four fifty-one. They throw out the big buoy that was built to be dropped at the North Pole. There's no message in it.
Ten fifty-three that night, alone on watch, Andrée writes: It is not a little strange to be floating here above the Polar Sea. The first to have floated here in a balloon.
We think we can well face death, having done what we have now done.
Thirty-three years later, an archbishop will read that sentence out loud in Stockholm.
That night, a rope snags on an ice block and holds the balloon for thirteen hours.
Tuesday, July thirteenth. Ten fifty-five in the morning, it tears free.
For lunch, Strindberg writes a menu: soup, Chateaubriand, beer and chocolate. A good and strengthening meal!
One oh eight in the afternoon. Four more pigeons. Andrée's message: All well on board. This is the third pigeon post.
Then the fog turns to drizzle, and the drizzle freezes on the balloon. In the end, about a ton of ice and water is pulling the Eagle down.
They throw out almost everything they can spare, including four hundred and forty pounds of food.
Wednesday, July fourteenth. Five twenty-eight in the morning. They open both valves.
Seven nineteen. Andrée writes: We jumped out of the gondola.
Sixty-five hours and thirty-three minutes in the air. Four hundred and eighty-eight miles short of the North Pole.
You'll read that the balloon crashed. It didn't. They brought it down themselves, and even the pigeons on board survived.
That's the moment Strindberg photographs it.
Thursday, July fifteenth. Andrée writes one line: Decided to start from the point where we were.
The nearest big food depot is at Cape Flora, about two hundred and ninety miles southeast, across moving ice.
They build sledges and test a canvas boat. Then, on Thursday, July twenty-second, they start walking.
Each sledge weighs up to four hundred and forty pounds, so all three pull one forward, then walk back for the next.
Over ridges of broken ice. Across cracks of open water, on the boat.
And underneath them, the ice is moving. In just three days, it carries them eight miles back north.
Wednesday, August fourth, one in the morning. Andrée writes: We can overcome neither the current nor the ice.
They turn around, toward a smaller depot on the Seven Islands, at the northern tip of Svalbard.
Now, those pigeons.
Thursday, July fifteenth, between one and two in the morning. The Norwegian sealing ship Alken is working the edge of the ice, a hundred and five miles from where the third post was released.
A strange bird lands on the rigging, exhausted, its head under its wing.
Up in the crow's nest, the harpooner says: Don't shoot it. You won't hit it anyway. And besides, it's a troll bird.
The skipper climbs up and shoots it. It falls into the sea, and nobody bothers to lower a boat.
Later, another skipper asks him: Could that have been one of Andrée's pigeons?
He turns back. They find the bird floating, with a little tube under a tail feather.
All well on board. This is the third pigeon post.
Thirty-six pigeons. That's the only one ever recovered, and its message got home by ship.
American papers printed a different message: Eighty-two degrees passed, good journey northward. It was a rumor.
And the American Arctic explorer Adolphus Greely told the Associated Press: I believe that Andrée will never return.
Back on the ice, the walk toward the Seven Islands goes no better.
Mid-September, a storm. Andrée: Our position is not particularly good. They decide to spend the winter on the ice.
Friday, September seventeenth. For the first time since July eleventh, they see land. An island that looks like one single block of ice. White Island.
You'll also read that they starved. They didn't. Andrée writes that they have meat until April.
The bear, he writes, is the polar explorer's best friend. They eat everything, except, of course, the bear liver.
They build a hut of snow and ice blocks, and call it Home.
Saturday, October second, five thirty in the morning. A crash and a roar, and water streams into the hut. The floe has split, right outside the wall.
Andrée: No one had lost courage. With such comrades one should be able to manage under practically any circumstances.
The ice has carried them right up to White Island.
Tuesday, October fifth. Strindberg writes: Moved ashore. Solid ground, for the first time since they lifted off.
Wednesday: Snowstorm. Reconnoitering.
In nineteen thirty, a press release misread that word as Resignation. It isn't.
Thursday: Moving. They drag the sledges two hundred yards inland, to a camp under a rock ridge.
Andrée's diary fades out in fragments. Strong wind. Darkness. The snow hut. Driftwood.
Then ten days with no entries at all.
Sunday, October seventeenth. Strindberg's almanac. Home, seven oh five a.m.
Home from where? Nobody knows. It's the last thing any of them wrote.
That day, the sun is up for about four hours. Three days later, it sets, and won't rise again until February.
Strindberg died first. The other two buried him in a narrow crack in the rock, about a hundred feet from the tent, under stones.
When Andrée and Frænkel died, nobody knows.
In Sweden, nobody knows any of this.
August eighteen ninety-eight. A Swedish scientific expedition lands on the southwest point of White Island.
If they'd walked a little over half a mile further inland, they would have stood where the three men lay.
They see nothing, and sail on.
Over the next two years, three of the expedition's buoys wash up. In Iceland, on an Arctic island, and in Norway.
For thirty-three years, the island keeps the rest.
Wednesday, August sixth, nineteen thirty. White Island. Brilliant sun, dead calm, in an unusually ice-free summer.
A Norwegian sealing ship, the Bratvaag, is anchored offshore.
Two young sealers, seventeen and twenty-four, walk up the beach looking for drinking water.
They pick up an aluminum lid.
Then they see something dark sticking out of a snowdrift. A canvas boat, half buried, full of gear. Marked: Andrée's polar expedition.
The skipper rows back to the ship, calm and quiet, and says: they've found Andrée.
They find the camp, and two of the three men. A stove on the rock still has kerosene in it. When they pump it, it works.
Why now? That summer, there was unusually little snow. A couple of feet more, the expedition's leader wrote, and all would have been hidden.
A month later, a second ship finds the third man. And a canvas bag of film canisters.
The film is Eastman Kodak. The date to develop it by was February, eighteen ninety-eight. It's now nineteen thirty.
The canisters had been soaked for years. The coating came off at a touch.
In Stockholm, the photographer John Hertzberg tests his chemicals on the blank rolls first. Then he develops the real ones.
About fifty frames show something. About twenty are clear enough to print. Later counts say ninety-three.
They're the photos you've been watching. The balloon on the ice. The sledges. A bear shot for food. Three men on a ridge of ice.
But the prints most people have seen were retouched in nineteen thirty. This is one before, and after.
Sunday, October fifth, nineteen thirty. Exactly thirty-three years after they stepped ashore on White Island.
The same navy gunboat that carried them north in eighteen ninety-seven brings them home to Stockholm.
Strindberg's engagement ring was found with him. In Andrée's pocket was a gold locket with Strindberg's fiancée's portrait.
In the cathedral, the archbishop reads the sentence Andrée wrote alone over the Polar Sea. We think we can well face death, having done what we have now done.
So how did they die? They had food, fuel, matches, guns and ammunition. No note and no diary entry describes an illness or a crisis.
Cold and exhaustion, people said in nineteen thirty. But early October wasn't the worst of the cold.
Trichinosis, from undercooked bear meat, said a Danish doctor in nineteen fifty-two. It became the famous answer. But trichinosis rarely kills.
Botulism. Lead poisoning. Fumes from the stove. Every one has been argued. None has been proven.
Vitamin A poisoning from bear liver? Andrée's own diary says they didn't eat the liver.
The leading modern theory comes from a Swedish doctor, Bea Uusma. In the museum's storage, she studied Strindberg's jacket, with large ragged tears, and his long underwear, with one leg missing.
She's convinced a polar bear killed Strindberg. How the other two died, she says, is still undetermined.
In twenty twenty-four, her team sailed back to White Island to search again. Polar bears kept them from landing.
And in nineteen thirty, the three men were cremated. No samples were kept.
Strindberg's tin, the one he dropped on Vogelsang that first afternoon, has never been reported found.
