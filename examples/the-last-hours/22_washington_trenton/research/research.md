# WASHINGTON CROSSES THE DELAWARE / THE LAST HOURS BEFORE THE REVOLUTION NEARLY DIED: verified research (Wednesday 25 to Thursday 26 December 1776. **About 4 p.m. Wed 25 Dec:** drums beat and the regiments march for McConkey's Ferry, Pennsylvania. **4:40 p.m.:** sunset. **6 p.m.:** Washington writes from the ferry: "the night is favourable." **At dusk:** the first boats push off into the ice. **About 11 p.m.:** a nor'easter hits. **Midnight:** the hour Washington had planned to have everything across. **About 2 a.m. Thu 26 Dec:** the infantry is all on the New Jersey side (Knox). **3 a.m.:** the last guns are over (Washington). **Near 4 a.m.:** the march begins, nine miles to Trenton, three hours late. **8:00 a.m. exactly:** the attack on the Hessian outpost (Washington); fire on the River Road three minutes later. **About 9 a.m.:** the Hessian brigade has surrendered. **Afternoon:** the army marches back and recrosses the river with its prisoners. **Fri 27 Dec, evening:** Colonel Rall dies of his wounds. **Tue 31 Dec:** the day most of the army's enlistments were due to run out.)

Channel: The Last Hours (@TheLastHoursDocs; US / Canada / UK audience, 35+, mostly on TV). Format: hour-by-hour countdown with the clock times the record has, told only from the record. Folder 22_washington_trenton. Narrator: the File Keeper. Length about 10-12 min. Release **Sunday 27 December 2026**, the 250th-anniversary week. Frame: "the last hours before the Revolution nearly died": an army whose enlistments ran out on 31 December, one night, one river.

Spine: **Sat 16 Nov 1776** Fort Washington falls (Manhattan) → **Wed 20 Nov** Fort Lee abandoned; the retreat across New Jersey → **Fri 22 Nov** at the Passaic, Thomas Paine resolves to write *The American Crisis* → **7-8 Dec** the army crosses into Pennsylvania; **Sun 8 Dec** the British enter Trenton → **Wed 18 Dec** Washington: "I think the game is pretty near up" → **Thu 19 Dec** *The American Crisis* No. 1 in the *Pennsylvania Journal* → **Fri 20 Dec** Washington to Congress: "ten days more will put an end to the existence of our Army" → **Mon 23 Dec** the *Crisis* as a pamphlet; Washington to Reed: "Christmas day at Night, one hour before day is the time fixed upon for our Attempt on Trenton" → **Tue 24 Dec (probably), morning** Benjamin Rush sees a slip on the floor: "victory or death"; late on 24 Dec, the last council of war → **Wed 25 Dec** the general orders; ~4 p.m. march to the river; dusk: the crossing begins; ~11 p.m. the storm → **Thu 26 Dec** 2 a.m. troops over; 3 a.m. guns over; ~4 a.m. march; 8:00 a.m. attack; ~9 a.m. surrender; afternoon: back across the river → **Fri 27 Dec** report to Congress; Rall dies → **Mon 30 Dec** Washington back in Trenton; New England regiments agree to stay six more weeks for a $10 bounty → **Tue 31 Dec** the enlistment deadline; Congress reads the report → **Thu 2 Jan 1777** Second Trenton (Assunpink Creek) → **Fri 3 Jan 1777** Princeton.

Compiled 6 Nov 2026. Release day (Sun 27 Dec 2026) is **91,311 days** after the battle (Thu 26 Dec 1776): 250 years and 1 day. The exact 250th anniversary of the crossing is **Friday 25 December 2026**; of the battle, **Saturday 26 December 2026** [COMPUTED: CAL].

**Clock note (read first):**
- **1776 clocks ran on local (sun) time.** There were no time zones. Local mean time at McConkey's Ferry (74.87°W) is UTC-4:59.5, so **a 1776 clock time at the Delaware equals today's Eastern Standard Time to within a minute**. "8 a.m. in Trenton" = 8 a.m. EST = **1 p.m. in London** (GMT) = 5 a.m. in Los Angeles (PST) [COMPUTED: TZ].
- Officers' watches were set by Washington's before the attack (Capt. William Hull, 1 Jan 1777, quoted in FO-0355 n.6; FISCHER p. 230). That is why the 8:00 a.m. attack and the "three minutes after" are believable to the minute.
- **The sun and moon (COMPUTED, McConkey's Ferry 40.299°N 74.874°W, ephem):** Wed 25 Dec sunset **4:40 p.m.** (FISCHER p. 209: 4:41); end of civil dusk **5:10 p.m.**; Thu 26 Dec civil dawn **6:51 a.m.**, sunrise **7:22 a.m.** (FISCHER p. 234: 7:20). **Full moon fell at 8:05 p.m. local time on Tue 24 Dec.** On the crossing night the moon (99% lit) rose about **5:30 p.m.**, stood about 58° high at 11 p.m. and 3 a.m., and set at **8:37 a.m.**, just as the attack began. **The storm hid it.** FISCHER p. 218 says the same: a bright moon rose after sunset but was obscured by the storm. Say "a full moon was up there, behind the storm", never "the moonlit river".
- Planned vs actual: across "by 12 OClock" → artillery over "three OClock" (**3 hours late**); march to arrive "at Trenton at five o'clock" → attack at **8:00** (**3 hours late**, well after sunrise) [RECORD: FO-0341; FO-0355].
- All clock times in this file are local Trenton/Delaware time (= EST) unless marked.

**Record note:** The night is told by (1) **Washington's own letters and orders on Founders Online (FO)**, read in full in the browser: the general orders of 25 Dec (Knox's draft), the 6 p.m. note to Cadwalader, the report to John Hancock of 27 Dec, and the letters of 18, 20, 23, 24 and 30 Dec; plus the editors' notes (Papers of George Washington, Revolutionary War Series, vol. 7, ed. Chase, 1997), which quote Knox, Hull, Johnston, Tilghman, Wiederhold, Rodney and Sgt John Smith; (2) **Fitzpatrick, *Writings of Washington*, vol. 6 (1932)**, full text on archive.org (draft variants; his doubt about the 23 Dec letter); (3) **John Greenwood's memoir** (written 1809, printed 1922), full text, archive.org: the fifer's night; (4) **Benjamin Rush's memoir** (*A Memorial*, 1905 printing), full text, archive.org: the "victory or death" slip; (5) **Stryker, *The Battles of Trenton and Princeton* (1898)**, full text, archive.org: the 19th-century standard account and the source of most legends; (6) **David Hackett Fischer, *Washington's Crossing* (Oxford UP, 2004)**, read by targeted full-text searches of the archive.org copy (page numbers are the printed pages; quotations from Fischer are paraphrased here, not copied); (7) Thomas Paine, *The American Crisis* No. 1 (Conway ed., Project Gutenberg); (8) **Washington Crossing Historic Park's own event pages (Dec 2026)**, read through the site's events API. **The Library of Congress site (loc.gov) was behind a bot check and was not opened** (section 15).

## How to read this file

Every fact carries a status tag and one or more source IDs (section 0).

- **[RECORD]**: primary: Washington's letters and orders (Founders Online text), Knox's and Rodney's letters of Dec 1776, Hessian diaries as quoted by the Washington Papers editors, Paine's 1776 text, the Hessian returns as summarised by Fischer's appendix.
- **[REPORTED]**: historians (Fischer, Stryker, Fitzpatrick's notes) and memoirs written years later (Greenwood 1809, Rush c. 1800, Wilkinson 1816, Bostwick, "Sergeant R—").
- **[CLAIM]**: one person's assertion that no second source supports.
- **[LEGEND]**: family or local tradition; historians reject or cannot document it.
- **[DISPUTED]**: sources disagree (each version given).
- **[COMPUTED]**: our arithmetic (weekdays, sun and moon, ages, intervals, distances). Method shown.
- **[UNKNOWN]**: the record we read is silent; what would settle it is named.
- **(FT)** = read in a full-text copy. **(FTS)** = found by full-text search (snippet with page number). **(browser)** = read on the live page. **WIKI** = Wikipedia, lead-finder only.

Rules kept: no invented numbers, quotes or sources; UNKNOWN where the record is silent. Quotes from 1776-1830 writers are public domain and quoted exactly. Fischer (2004) and the modern editors' notes are paraphrased with page/note numbers.

**Content flags for the edit (hard rules):**
- **Women and girls: no images, ever.** Narration-only mentions in this story: Lucy Flucker Knox (Knox's letter is to her), Mary Katherine Goddard (the Baltimore printer of Washington's report, 31 Dec 1776), Rebecca Coxe (nursed a wounded American in Trenton), Mary Dagworthy (later Mrs Abraham Hunt), the young widow at Mount Holly who kept Colonel von Donop away from Trenton (FISCHER p. 200), the Trenton woman who fired from a window (FISCHER p. 249). **Leutze's painting:** the Met's own audio guide says the boat holds every kind of American "except for women", **but** one rower behind Washington (red coat, long curly hair, rowing at the stern side) is read by some modern viewers as a woman. Treat that figure as AT RISK: use crops that leave him out (assets.md gives them). Never use: Currier's "Washington's Reception by the Ladies" (1845), Peale's Cadwalader family portrait, any reenactment photo with women.
- **No wounds on screen.** Rall's and Monroe's wounds: one line of narration, no detail. The frozen dead on the road: narration only.
- **No modern reenactor photos as if they were 1776.** If used at all (assets.md: only PD/CC items), label "reenactment, [year]".
- **No AI images.** Pexels clips checked for AI look (assets.md).
- **Myths:** the drunk Hessians, the unread note, the spy Honeyman, the "Fitzgerald diary" quotes, the Leutze flag: never as fact (section 7).

## 5. The mechanism: why one night mattered (facts only)

1. The army was ending by contract: the eastern regiments' terms ran out on 31 Dec (FO-0385); Washington (20 Dec): "ten days more" (FO-0305).
2. He chose an attack at the one place the Hessians were isolated: Donop had wandered to Mount Holly, 18 miles away (FISCHER pp. 199-200).
3. Three crossings were planned; only one got over (FO-0355).
4. The storm made the crossing three hours late but also hid the approach: Wiederhold's dawn patrol had come back reporting all quiet, and his sentries were no longer sharp "as it was broad daylight" (FO-0355 n.6; FISCHER pp. 234-236).
5. The win came just before the deadline; on 30 Dec the New England regiments agreed to six more weeks for $10 (FO-0385).

## 7. What the record shows / what it doesn't (MYTH vs RECORD)

| Myth / common line | Record | Status |
|---|---|---|
| **"The Hessians were drunk after their Christmas party."** | Greenwood guarded the prisoners and swore he saw not one drunk; the Rall regiment was the duty regiment, on alert all night; the garrison had been turned out by alarms on 22, 23 and 25 Dec; the other regiments slept in uniform. The drunk-Hessian story was spread by British officers looking for someone to blame (Stephen Kemble called Rall "a Drunkard"). Fischer: no strong evidence anyone was drunk | Myth. RECORD/REPORTED: GREENWOOD p. 40; FISCHER pp. 204, 240, 426 |
| "Rall was playing cards / checkers all night and was drunk" | Checkers with Potts and cards at Hunt's are local tradition (Stryker); Rall was up for the 7-8 p.m. alarm and was woken at 8 a.m. | LEGEND (games) / REPORTED (woken) |
| **"A Loyalist's warning note was found unread in Rall's pocket."** | Stryker (1898) tells it (Rall: "If I had read this at Mr. Hunt's I would not be here"). Greenwood's 1922 editor: the letter "is said to have been found, unopened". No 1776 document has it. Rall **was** warned verbally several times (Fischer) | LEGEND (note); REPORTED (warnings). STRYKER pp. 125, 192; FISCHER p. 204 |
| "John Honeyman, Washington's spy, lulled Rall" | Family legend; no evidence (Fischer's appendix) | LEGEND. FISCHER p. 423 |
| **"The diary of Col. John Fitzgerald"** ("It will be a terrible night for the soldiers who have no shoes", "Tell General Sullivan to use the bayonet", 23 Dec "Victory or Death") | First printed by Stryker (1898) as "Diary of an Officer on Washington's Staff", no source given; attributed to Fitzgerald only in 1957 (Scheer & Rankin). ## 10. Safe lines (accurate phrasings the script may use)

- "The orders were simple: profound silence; no man to leave the ranks, on pain of death; officers with a piece of white paper in their hats." (FO-0341)
- "At six in the evening, at the ferry, he wrote: 'the night is favourable.' By eleven, a nor'easter had arrived." (FO-0343; FISCHER p. 212)
- "Above the storm there was a full moon. Nobody saw it." (COMPUTED; FISCHER p. 218)
- "Two other forces were supposed to cross that night. The ice stopped both." (FO-0355)
- "At eight o'clock exactly, by his own watch, the first shots." (FO-0355; Hull)

## 11. Do-not-say list

- "The Hessians were drunk / hung over" (myth; section 7).
- "Rall died with an unread warning in his pocket" as fact (legend: "the story goes").
- Any line from the "Fitzgerald diary".
- "They crossed at midnight"; "under the moonlight".
- "Washington stood heroically in the bow" as fact (Leutze's invention).
- "The flag in the boat" as historical (Stars and Stripes: June 1777).
- "Washington ordered Paine read to the troops" as fact.
- "No Americans died".
- "The Revolution was saved" as a flat claim: say "the army survived its deadline" / "historians call it the turning point".
- Judgement words about Hessians ("mercenaries" is fine as a 1776 term if attributed; avoid "thugs", "hirelings" except in quotes).
