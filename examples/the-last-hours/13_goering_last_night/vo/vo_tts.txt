Imagine you search Hermann Goering a hundred times, and watch him twenty-four hours a day. About two hours before his hanging, he's dead. But how?
Tuesday, October fifteenth, nineteen forty-six. Ten forty-five at night. Nuremberg prison, Germany.
Two weeks earlier, the court had sentenced him to hang. The hangings were set for the early hours of the next morning.
A guard stood outside his door day and night. The light in his cell stayed on. He had to sleep with his hands outside the blanket.
And still, that night, they found pieces of glass in his mouth.
By the end of this file, you'll know what the guard saw, what Goering's own note said, what the Army decided, and why four men later claimed they knew the real answer.
##
Hermann Goering had been Hitler's chosen successor, and the head of the German Air Force.
In July nineteen forty-five, an American Army historian asked him one question. Why did Germany declare war on the United States?
His first words: I was astonished.
Tuesday, October first, nineteen forty-six. The judges said his guilt was unique in its enormity.
Then the words: the International Military Tribunal sentences you to death by hanging.
Eleven men got the same sentence. Goering asked to be shot instead of hanged.
By Thursday, October tenth, every appeal had been turned down.
The next day, he took a pencil and wrote a note to the prison commandant. Remember that date. Friday, October eleventh. It matters later.
##
The commandant was an American colonel, Burton Andrus. And he had already lost two prisoners.
In October nineteen forty-five, Leonardo Conti hanged himself in his cell. Less than three weeks later, Robert Lye killed himself too.
After that, every prisoner got a guard of his own, at his door, around the clock.
Every thirty seconds, the guard looked in.
The tables were built to collapse under a man's weight. Razors, neckties and shoelaces were taken away.
The cells were searched while the men were out exercising. By the Associated Press's count, Goering was searched about a hundred times.
Andrus had said a suicide in his prison was just about impossible.
##
By Monday, October fourteenth, the condemned men had been told officially. No appeals left.
They were not told the date. But October sixteenth had been in the newspapers for two weeks.
Tuesday, October fifteenth. Goering refuses his exercise period.
He says he has no fear, and that he'll try to show dignity to the last.
At five twenty-six, the sun goes down over Nuremberg.
Around eight o'clock, the prison chaplain, Henry Gerecky, comes to his cell. They talk for half an hour.
Goering asks for communion. Gerecky refuses.
At nine, the prison lights are dimmed.
Around ten, Goering seems to be asleep on his cot.
Thirty-five yards away, in the prison gymnasium, the Army is ready for the morning.
Three days earlier, American guards had played basketball in that room. Hold on to it. We'll be back there at one eleven in the morning.
##
At the cell door, the guard watches through a small hatch.
Goering's hands stay on top of the blanket. The guard never sees them go to his mouth.
Then he hears an odd noise, and sees Goering twitch.
He calls for help. The chaplain and the German prison doctor are close by, in the corridor.
They find him dying.
Ten forty-five at night. Hermann Goering is dead. He had taken cyanide.
In his mouth, they find pieces of glass.
Beside him, a small metal case, made from a rifle cartridge.
And an envelope, marked in pencil: H. Goering. Inside are his last letters. One is for Colonel Andrus.
At that moment, Andrus is crossing the prison courtyard, on his way to the cell.
The next day he tells reporters: I am completely baffled by it.
##
Remember the date on that note? Friday, October eleventh. Four days earlier.
The Army's English copy reads: I have always had the capsule of poison with me, from the time that I became a prisoner.
He says he'd had three capsules. The first, he left in his clothes, so that it would be found when a search was made.
The second, he says, he kept on him. During the court sessions, in his high riding boots.
The third, he writes, is still in his small suitcase, in the round box of skin cream.
And then: none of those charged with searching is to be blamed. It was practically impossible to find the capsule.
Eleven days after his death, a four-power commission agreed. He had probably had it all along.
Maybe inside his body. Maybe in his navel. Maybe under the rim of the toilet.
And no prison guard was to blame.
That was the official answer. It didn't stay the only answer for long.
##
In nineteen fifty-one, a former S.S. general who had testified at Nuremberg said he'd smuggled the poison in, inside a bar of soap.
American officials believed him. But the only evidence was his own word.
In nineteen eighty-five, a book blamed an American lieutenant who ran the prison's baggage room. Goering had given him a gold watch.
In two thousand five, a former guard, then seventy-eight, said he had carried medicine to Goering, hidden in a fountain pen. He said he never knew what was inside.
Military records show he really was a guard. Nothing else could be checked.
And in twenty twenty-six, a new book claims only six men ever knew what happened, and that four of them took the secret to their graves.
A bar of soap. A lieutenant. A fountain pen. Six men.
Not one of these stories has ever been proved.
##
Now, back to that gymnasium.
Wednesday, October sixteenth. One eleven in the morning. The first condemned man walks in. Joachim von Ribbentrop, Hitler's foreign minister.
About thirty witnesses are inside, including eight reporters, two from each of the four powers, chosen by lot.
One by one, the ten men are brought in.
At two forty-five, the tenth man is brought in. The Army hangman would later say: ten men in a hundred and three minutes.
At two fifty-four, the guards carry in a stretcher, under an army blanket. On it is Hermann Goering.
The blanket is pulled back, so the witnesses can see for themselves that he is dead.
At three in the morning, he is covered again.
##
The eleven bodies were driven in two vans to Munich, about ninety-five miles south, and burned there.
On Thursday, October seventeenth, American soldiers poured the ashes into a small creek in Munich, about half a mile long.
No grave. No marker.
Goering's notes were not published. The Control Council kept them sealed.
Colonel Andrus left the prison that December. He reportedly blamed himself for the rest of his life.
And Douglas Kelley, the Army psychiatrist who had studied Goering in that prison, took cyanide too, on New Year's Day, nineteen fifty-eight.
##
So here's what the record shows.
He died at ten forty-five at night, about two and a half hours before the first man walked into that gymnasium.
His note said he'd had the poison all along. The Army said the same.
Four men later said they knew better. Not one of them has ever been proved right.
