# USS CYCLOPS (Fuel Ship No. 4, later listed as AC-4): verified research (Navy collier lost with all aboard after leaving Barbados, Monday 4 March 1918)

Channel: The Last Hours (@TheLastHoursDocs; US / Canada / UK audience). Format: hour-by-hour (here: day-by-day) countdown told only from the record. Folder 10_cyclops, release date: owner to set.
Spine: Norfolk (8 Jan 1918) → Rio de Janeiro (arrives about 28 Jan; loads about 10,800 tons of manganese ore; starboard engine already crippled) → sails 16 Feb → Bahia (20-22 Feb) → the unscheduled stop at Barbados (arrives 5:30 p.m. Sunday 3 March "for bunker coal") → the last message (filed 7:00 a.m., received in Washington 11:45 a.m., Monday 4 March) → departure about 5 p.m. Monday 4 March, "she steamed south" → silence; a gale off the Virginia Capes 9-10 March → due at Baltimore Wednesday 13 March, never arrives → "recorded as missing" 23 March → the Navy tells the families and the press, Sunday 14 April → search, spy rumours, U-boat theories → official date of loss 14 June 1918 → Germany knows nothing (Kiel, 14 Dec 1918) → Congress pays the families' insurance (1919) → 1941: two sister ships vanish the same way → 1968: a Navy diver's wreck off Virginia, never identified → 2026: still not found.
Compiled 2026-10-24.

**Clock note (read first):** There is no minute-by-minute log for this story: no radio traffic exists after Barbados. The "hours" are the few clock times in the record: Worley's arrival cable (1730, 3 March), its filing group (0700, 4 March) and its receipt stamp in Washington ("1145 AM 3-4-18" = 11:45 a.m., 4 March), and the consul's son's memory of "about five o'clock" for the departure. Time zones in 1918 [REPORTED: TZ]: **Barbados kept local mean time, about UTC−3:58** (it did not adopt Atlantic Standard Time until 1932); **Rio and Bahia: UTC−3** (Brazil's standard time from 1914); **Washington and Baltimore: Eastern Standard Time, UTC−5**. The United States had **no daylight saving time until Sunday 31 March 1918** (Standard Time Act of 19 March 1918) [REPORTED: STA1918; weekday COMPUTED], so the overdue date (13 March) was EST and the Navy's announcement (14 April) fell in America's first-ever daylight time. Navy message groups of 1918 such as "17303" are read as time + day (1730 on the 3rd) [COMPUTED; see 4.13].

**Record note:** The Navy never convened a published court of inquiry that we could find; its "findings" are the overdue notice, the telegrams to families, the June 1918 declaration of loss, the Office of Naval Intelligence (ONI) investigation (reported, not online), Secretary Daniels's later account, and NHHC's modern summaries. The Navy's Cyclops files at the National Archives (RG 45 / ONI, said to run to about 1,500 pages) are **not online** and were not read (section 19).

## How to read this file

Every fact carries a status tag and one or more source IDs (section 0).

- **[CONFIRMED]**: primary/official record: NHHC DANFS and NHHC documentary-history transcriptions of National Archives documents; NARA catalog documents and photographs (Worley's personnel file, Bureau of Ships plans, Navy Department photo cards); U.S. government text printed in 1918 newspapers (the Bureau of Navigation telegram); the Navy dispatch printed by Secretary Daniels; the Congressional Record; U.S. Navy publications (1920 U-boat history; Surgeon General's report).
- **[REPORTED]**: credible secondary: NHHC H-Grams and articles, USNI *Proceedings* and *Naval History* (including first-hand accounts by Nervig, Tisdale, Hawes and the consul's son), *Popular Science* 1929, 1918 AP/press reports, NUMA, Barrash, Quasar. Wikipedia used only as a lead-finder and tagged WIKI.
- **[DISPUTED]**: sources disagree (both sides given).
- **[COMPUTED]**: our arithmetic from confirmed inputs (weekdays, sun/moon, distances, speeds, unit conversions, intervals). Method shown.
- **[LEGEND]**: rumour, myth, hoax, or popular error. Do not narrate as fact.

Rules kept: no invented numbers, quotes or sources. Long quotes only from U.S. government text (public domain). Modern writers paraphrased; any modern quote under 15 words.

**Content flags for the edit (hard rules):**
- **About 306 people are presumed dead** (the count varies, 293-312: section 1). Factual, respectful tone. No bodies, no wreckage: nothing to describe. No speculation presented as fact.
- **Women and girls: no images, ever.** No woman or girl is named among those aboard in any source we read [REPORTED]. Women in the story (mothers, wives, sisters, Worley's wife and daughter, sponsors of the sister ships) are **narration only**. List in 3.4. **Exclude** the well-known photo of Worley with his daughter (used by USNI, credited NHHC).
- **Lt. Cmdr. George W. Worley's dignity:** he was suspected of treason, mutiny-provoking cruelty and drinking. The record shows a German birth he hid, harsh command, and an "Excellent" Navy fitness report in 1917. **No evidence of disloyalty was ever found** (ONI, Daniels, NHHC). Rumours stay rumours (section 6).
- **No Bermuda Triangle framing as cause.** The name did not exist until 1964. Say what the record shows; name the myths as myths.
- **Not "the Navy's largest non-combat loss" as a flat fact.** NHHC's own Director disputes it (section 6 #1). Safe: "the U.S. Navy's deadliest single loss of World War I".

## 8. KNOWN vs THEORY

### 8.1 Known (record)
- She left Rio on 16 Feb 1918 with about 10,800 tons of manganese ore and a crippled starboard engine. [CONFIRMED: DANFS / REPORTED: RECK29; NERVIG69]
- She arrived at Barbados, unscheduled, at 5:30 p.m. on Sunday 3 March "for bunker coal", and cabled that she would reach Baltimore on 13 March. [CONFIRMED: DANIELS22]
- That cable, received at 11:45 a.m. Monday 4 March, is the last word from her. She sailed that evening. [CONFIRMED / REPORTED (time of sailing)]
- She never reached Baltimore; no radio call, wreckage or body was ever found. [CONFIRMED]
- Germany had no record of her. [CONFIRMED: DH-ROB]
- The Navy dated the loss 14 June 1918. [CONFIRMED: NARA-SC]
