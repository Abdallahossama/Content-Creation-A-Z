Imagine you're one of three hundred and six people lost at sea in nineteen eighteen. Your last message is about coal. But nobody ever finds a single plank.
Monday, March fourth, nineteen eighteen. Eleven forty-five in the morning.
In Washington, a Navy office stamps a short cable from the island of Barbados.
Arrived Barbados, West Indies, for bunker coal. Arrive Baltimore, the thirteenth.
That evening, the U S S Cyclops sounds her whistle, backs out of the bay, and, as one witness remembered it, steams south. Baltimore is north.
She is never seen again.
Forget the Bermuda Triangle. The Navy kept the telegrams, her captain's file, even the ship's own stability curves.
By the end, you'll know what she was carrying, who her captain really was, and why nobody can say what happened to her.
##
Sunday, March third, nineteen eighteen. Five thirty in the evening. A Navy collier anchors in Carlisle Bay, Barbados.
She isn't supposed to be here. The stop is not on her schedule.
A collier is a ship built to carry coal to the fleet. This one is five hundred forty-two feet long, about one and a half football fields.
But on this trip, she isn't carrying coal. She's carrying about ten thousand eight hundred tons of manganese ore from Brazil, for American steel.
This ore is far heavier than coal for its size. Remember the ore.
By the Navy's count, three hundred and six people are aboard. Her crew. Dozens of sailors going home from ships off South America. Five prisoners. And the American consul general from Rio de Janeiro.
The American consul in Barbados, a man named Livingston, comes aboard. The captain wants coal, and money to pay for it.
He also takes on a ton of fresh meat, a ton of flour, and a thousand pounds of vegetables. Seven hundred seventy-five dollars, the consul writes.
And the consul thinks something is off. By his reckoning, the ship probably already had more than fifteen hundred tons of coal on board.
Remember the coal on board.
##
That morning, the captain files his cable to Washington. It reaches the Navy at eleven forty-five.
It's the last word anyone will ever receive from the Cyclops.
In the afternoon, the captain and two others from the ship visit the consulate.
Around five, they go back aboard. A few blasts on the whistle. The Cyclops backs out and, the consul's son remembered years later, steams south.
Baltimore lies to the north. Nobody on the beach thinks anything of it. Not for a few weeks.
Her captain is Lieutenant Commander George Worley, fifty-one by his own papers. He's been her master since she was built in nineteen ten.
Who he really was, we'll get to.
##
Rewind to January. The Cyclops leaves Norfolk, Virginia, loaded to her marks with coal and mail for the Navy's ships off South America.
One of her officers, Conrad Nervig, will remember the trip for the rest of his life.
On the first night, he hears the hull grinding and sees the deck rising and falling with the waves. The captain tells him: Son, she'll last as long as we do.
Five days out, the captain puts his own second-in-command under arrest in his cabin, after a trivial disagreement.
Nervig remembers the captain visiting the night watch in long woolen underwear, a derby hat, and a cane.
Then, on the way to Rio, the head of a cylinder in the starboard engine blows off. From then on, she runs on one engine.
Remember the engine.
##
Rio de Janeiro, late January. A Navy survey board looks at her engines and finds them not in satisfactory condition. Repairs are put off until she gets home.
Nervig is transferred to another ship. That's the only reason we have his story.
Into her holds goes the ore. A later Navy report: ten thousand eight hundred thirty-five tons, stowed directly on timber in the bottom of the hold.
And four thousand tons of water, mostly in the double bottom.
Saturday, February sixteenth. She leaves Rio, calls at Bah-ee-ah, and heads north for home.
##
So who was George Worley?
In March, nineteen seventeen, he filled out his Navy medical form. Where born: San Francisco, California. Date of birth: December eleventh, eighteen sixty-six.
After the ship vanished, Navy investigators reported something else. He had been born in Germany, under another name, and had jumped ship as a young man.
In the middle of a war with Germany, that was enough. The consul in Barbados cabled Washington that the other officers called the captain a damned Dutchman.
He wrote: I fear fate worse than sinking. Then he admitted it might come from an instinctive dislike of the man.
You'll read that Worley was a drunk and a traitor who handed the ship to the Kaiser. Here's the record.
His last Navy fitness report rates him excellent. Intemperance: no. Would the Navy keep him? Yes, highly so.
The Navy's intelligence office found no evidence that he was disloyal. And after the war, Germany would have its own answer. We'll get there.
##
Back to Barbados, and the evening of March fourth. From here to the mouth of Chesapeake Bay is nearly two thousand miles of open Atlantic. About nine days, at her speed.
The Cyclops has a wireless radio. Not one message comes from it.
Saturday, March ninth. A molasses steamer, the Amolco, heading north to Boston, runs into a heavy storm off the coast.
Her boats are smashed, her bridge is wrecked, and her radio room floods.
The Weather Bureau later mapped it, from ships' reports: a storm off the Virginia Capes on March tenth. On those nights, there was almost no moon.
Wednesday, March thirteenth. The Cyclops is due in Baltimore. She doesn't come.
##
For ten days, radio stations call her across the Atlantic. Ships are told to watch for wreckage. On March twenty-third, she is recorded as missing.
Sunday, April fourteenth. The Navy tells the public, and sends telegrams to the families.
They read: Disappearance cannot be accounted for, as there has been no bad weather nor fighting in the ship's zone.
No bad weather. But later analysis found a front across her likely route on March eighth, and the gale off Virginia a day later.
A Navy official told reporters: the whole thing is an enigma.
##
Then the rumors start.
Worley sailed her to Germany. A secret agent saw her in the Keel Canal. Her crew were in a German prison camp. A spy was hidden in her coal at Barbados.
A newspaper asked if giant squids had devoured her. In August, a bottle turned up near Baltimore with a note: Captured by German submarine off the coast of Virginia.
The Navy checked every story. Every one was unfounded.
And a U-boat? The first German submarine to reach American waters that year left Germany on April fourteenth, six weeks after the Cyclops vanished.
Saturday, December fourteenth, nineteen eighteen. The war is over. At Keel, a German admiral tells American officers that no information about her fate is to be had in Germany.
##
By then, the Navy had given her up. It dated the loss June fourteenth, nineteen eighteen.
In Steelton, Pennsylvania, Andrew Askin's mother got the Navy's telegram. He was a mess attendant, twenty-two.
Some families couldn't even collect their sons' back pay. Less than a third of the crew had war-risk insurance.
In nineteen nineteen, the Senate agreed to treat every man aboard as insured for five thousand dollars.
No single loss cost the U S Navy more lives in World War One.
##
So what happened? The record supports three explanations, and it can't choose between them.
One. She broke in two. Ore sits low and heavy in a hull built for lighter coal. Nervig had heard her plates grinding. Years later, a similar Navy collier's steel beams were found eaten away by rust.
Two. She rolled over. In nineteen sixteen, a Navy officer aboard watched her roll fifty-six degrees, with almost nothing in her holds.
He believed a shifting load and loose water could turn her upside down. A capsize that fast would explain why nobody called for help.
Three. The storm. The same gale that nearly sank the Amolco, off Virginia.
A Navy report after the loss guessed a sudden shift of cargo. The Secretary of the Navy later wrote that it was all conjecture.
And then, twenty-three years later, it happened again. Twice.
##
Two of her sister ships, Proteus and Neer-ee-us, had been sold off. In late nineteen forty-one, they both sailed from the Virgin Islands, seventeen days apart, loaded with heavy ore.
Neither was ever seen again. No U-boat ever claimed them.
In nineteen sixty-eight, a Navy diver, Dean Hawes, went down a hundred and eighty feet onto a wreck about seventy miles off the mouth of Chesapeake Bay.
He saw a very old cargo ship with two cradled booms forward.
Later he wrote: I cannot say that the ship I saw was definitely the Cyclops. Nobody has proven it since.
##
You'll read that she vanished in the Bermuda Triangle. That name didn't exist until nineteen sixty-four. And the storm in the record was off Virginia.
You'll also read she was the Navy's biggest loss ever outside combat. The Navy's own historians say a frigate in eighteen hundred and a destroyer in a nineteen forty-four typhoon lost more.
##
So here's what the record can't tell us.
Where she is. When she went down. Whether she broke, rolled, or both.
Why she stopped at Barbados for coal she may not have needed. And why, as one witness remembered it, she steamed south.
Even how many were aboard. The records range from two hundred ninety-three to three hundred twelve.
At eleven forty-five on a Monday morning in nineteen eighteen, a Navy office stamped a cable about coal.
The Navy has their names, their pay records, their ship's plans, and a file on their captain. In a hundred and eight years, it has never found a single plank.
These are some of the men of the U S S Cyclops.
