Imagine you come back to your lighthouse, but your three workmates are gone.
They were never found, and nobody knows why.
Wednesday, December twenty-sixth, nineteen hundred. About noon.
A supply steamer, the Hesperus, drops anchor under a rock in the Atlantic, twenty miles off the Isle of Lewis, in Scotland.
On top of the cliff stands a lighthouse. There should be a flag flying on its pole. There isn't.
The captain sounds the steam whistle. Then the siren.
Then he fires a rocket. Nothing.
A boat is backed into the landing, and one man jumps for the rock. Joseph Moore, a keeper, coming back from his time ashore.
He climbs the stairs up the cliff. The gate is closed. The outside doors are closed.
In the kitchen, nobody has lit the fire for days. The clock has stopped.
He goes through the rooms, one by one. The beds are empty.
I did not take time to search further, he wrote, for I only too well knew something serious had occurred.
And he ran.
You may have heard this story before. A logbook where one keeper is crying, and they all pray, and the last line says, God is over all.
That logbook is fake. It isn't in a single record from nineteen hundred. It was made up decades later.
The real records are stranger. A note chalked on a slate. A coat left on its peg. And a stone of more than a ton, moved by the sea.
This is what those records say happened, hour by hour. And by the end, you'll know which parts of the famous story are true, and which were made up.
Saturday, December fifteenth, nineteen hundred. Nine in the morning. Eleven days before Moore lands.
On Eilean Mor, the big island of the Flannan Isles, three men are keeping the light.
James Ducat, the principal keeper. In his forties, with a wife and four children ashore.
Thomas Marshall, his assistant. About thirty, not married.
And Donald McArthur, an old army man from Lewis, standing in for a keeper who's off sick.
Ducat picks up a piece of chalk and writes on a slate. The barometer. The thermometer. The wind.
It's the last thing any of them will ever write.
Eight days ago, Ducat said something to his boss that will sound very different by Boxing Day.
The light above them is new. It was lit for the first time a year and a week ago.
Twenty miles of open water to the nearest point of Lewis. And due west, nothing at all until Canada.
There is no land, a newspaper wrote, between the Flannan Islands and America.
The morning work gets done. The lamp is trimmed. The oil tanks are filled. The lens and the machinery are cleaned. The blinds are drawn on the lantern windows.
At midday, they eat dinner. Then whoever is cooking that day washes the pots and pans, and tidies the kitchen.
Remember that kitchen. It matters later.
Nobody saw what happened next. But the things they left behind tell us a lot.
Sometime in the afternoon, two of the men get dressed to go out.
Marshall pulls on his sea boots and his oilskins. Ducat, his sea boots and an old waterproof coat.
The keepers only wore those things for one job. Going down to the landings, where the boats come in.
The island has two. One on the east side, one on the west.
The west landing sits at the end of a narrow gully. Above it, there's a platform with a crane, seventy feet above the sea.
The first crane on that platform didn't make it through the first winter. The sea took it.
And the third man, McArthur, goes out too. But his coat stays on its peg.
In December. In the North Atlantic. In his shirtsleeves.
The rules said one man must always stay with the light. But that rule was for the night. It's daytime.
When Moore arrives, he'll find the gate and the outside doors closed. Nobody knows who closed them.
Three thirty-nine in the afternoon. The sun goes down.
This is when the lamp gets lit. Every single day. It's trimmed, it's full, it's ready.
Nobody lights it.
Midnight. A steamer called the Archtor, out of Philadelphia and bound for Scotland, passes the Flannan Isles.
The night is clear, but stormy. There's no moon yet.
Captain Holman looks for the light. He can't see it.
From the weather and his position, he said, he was satisfied that he should have seen it.
His report won't reach the lighthouse board until the disaster is already in the newspapers.
About twenty miles away, on Lewis, a gamekeeper is paid eight pounds a year to watch for the light.
His two teenage sons do the watching. They saw it on the twelfth. Since then, nothing.
That's not unusual. In haze, the light can disappear for four or five nights at a time.
But the nights go on. Six. Seven. Eight.
The boys get anxious. They ask their neighbors to help them look.
No word reaches Edinburgh.
Thursday, the twentieth. A huge gale hits Scotland, and wrecks part of the Shetland fishing fleet.
The relief boat is due around the twenty-first. The weather keeps it away.
On Christmas Eve, the Hesperus finally sails, with Moore on board.
Christmas Day. The light stays dark.
Eleven nights.
Back to Boxing Day. Early afternoon.
Moore runs down to the landing and shouts to the boat.
The second mate and one of the seamen jump ashore, and the three of them search the station again. Together.
Up in the lightroom, everything is in order. The lamp was cleaned, Moore wrote. The fountain full. Blinds on the windows.
Moore will later list what's missing.
Marshall's sea boots and oilskins, gone. Ducat's boots and old waterproof coat, gone.
McArthur's everyday coat is still here.
He never used any other coat, Moore wrote. Only the one I am referring to.
The captain can't stay. Night is coming on.
Moore goes back up to the lighthouse with three volunteers from the ship, to keep the light burning.
Three forty-three. Sunset. Moore lights the lamp, in the proper time that night.
Twenty miles away, the boys on Lewis see it. The first light in eleven nights.
That evening, the captain sends a telegram to Edinburgh.
A dreadful accident has happened at Flannans.
The three keepers, Ducat, Marshall and the occasional, have disappeared from the island.
Poor fellows, they must have been blown over the cliffs, or drowned trying to secure a crane, or something like that.
The next day, Moore and the volunteers cross the island from end to end.
The east landing hasn't been touched. Its ropes are still coiled, just as they were left at the last relief.
On the west side, it's different.
A wooden box of mooring ropes was wedged into a crack in the rocks, a hundred and ten feet above the sea. It's gone.
The iron railings along the path have been torn from their foundations, and twisted.
Saturday, December twenty-ninth. Early morning. The superintendent lands. Robert Muirhead.
The sea is still too heavy to reach the landing. He climbs down to the crane platform, seventy feet up.
The crane is fine. Its arm is lowered and lashed to the rock. There's no sign anyone was working on it.
The ropes from the box are lying in the rocks, tangled round the crane's legs. But every single coil is still fastened.
A life buoy hung on a railing up there, a hundred and ten feet above the water. The sea tore it off its ropes.
And a block of stone weighing more than a ton has been ripped from higher up, and dropped on the concrete path.
Something hit this side of the island, very high, and very hard.
But nobody can say when. On the fifteenth? Or in the great gale, five days later?
Muirhead knew these men. He'd chosen them. He worked beside them on this rock for over a month, the summer before the light was lit.
And three weeks ago, on Friday, December seventh, he was here for the last relief.
I was the last person, he wrote, to shake hands with them and bid them adieu.
That day, Ducat told him something.
He said he wished he'd have to hoist one of the signals, just to see how soon it would be seen ashore, and how soon anyone would act on it.
Eight days later, the light went dark. And for eleven nights, nobody acted.
On January eighth, nineteen oh one, Muirhead sends in his report.
All three men, he decides, went down near the west landing, probably to secure that box of ropes.
And an unexpectedly large roller came up on the island, and swept them away with resistless force.
The wind can't have done it, he wrote. It was blowing from the west. It would have pushed them up the island, not into the sea.
Nobody saw it happen. No bodies were ever found. And Crown lawyers decided an inquiry wasn't necessary.
But his answer leaves the same question Moore had.
Why did all three go? And why did McArthur go without his coat?
A keeper who served here in the nineteen fifties, Walter Aldebert, had a theory. Two waves.
The first takes one man. The other runs back up for help. McArthur grabs a rope and runs out without his coat, and a second wave takes them both.
Ducat's daughter, interviewed in nineteen ninety at the age of ninety-eight, remembered something else.
Months earlier, the keepers had been fined five shillings after storm damage at the west landing.
Maybe, she thought, when the wind eased, they went down to check, and McArthur ran out to warn them.
Others point to the gully under the west landing. It ends in a cave, and in a big sea, water can burst up out of it.
And on this coast, storm waves have thrown boulders onto cliff tops more than a hundred and sixty feet above the sea.
None of it is proof. It's the best anyone has.
So where did the crying and the praying come from?
January fifth, nineteen oh one. Ten days after Moore landed, a local paper, the Oban Times, prints a new detail.
A half-finished breakfast on the table. And the chairs pushed aside, as if the men had jumped up and run.
Remember that kitchen? The official report says the pots and pans were clean, and the kitchen tidy.
Nineteen twelve. A poet turns the story into a famous poem, with a meal left on the table, a chair knocked over, and three black birds.
Nineteen twenty-nine. An American pulp magazine called True Strange Stories runs a piece under the name Ernest Fallon.
Nineteen sixty-five. An American writer, Vincent Gaddis, prints the log entries in a book. Ducat irritable. McArthur crying. The prayers. God is over all.
He even gets the ship's name wrong. He calls it the Archer.
The researcher Mike Dash traced those entries back to that nineteen twenty-nine story, and found evidence it was fiction.
And the real records rule it out anyway. The real log stops on the thirteenth. The last note, on the slate, is nine in the morning on the fifteenth. In Ducat's hand. And it's the weather.
In nineteen seventy-one, the lighthouse was automated. No keeper has lived on the rock since.
On Lewis, by the shore where their families lived, there's a memorial now. A bronze wave, with three names on it.
This summer, Joseph Moore's own letter went on public display in Edinburgh, for the first time.
A hundred and twenty-five years later, there's still no body, no witness, and no log.
On the morning of December fifteenth, nineteen hundred, James Ducat chalked the weather on a slate.
It's the last thing the three of them left us. The sea has never given back anything else.
