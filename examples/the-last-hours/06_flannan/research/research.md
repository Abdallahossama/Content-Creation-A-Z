# FLANNAN: verified research (the Flannan Isles lighthouse keepers, Eilean Mòr, December 1900)

Channel: The Last Hours (US / Canada / UK audience). Format: hour-by-hour / day-by-day countdown told from the original records. Folder 06_flannan, release target Sat 31 Oct 2026 (Halloween).
Spine: the 22 days from the last relief (Fri 7 Dec 1900) to Superintendent Muirhead's day on the island (Sat 29 Dec 1900), told from four documents written within two weeks of the discovery: Captain Harvie's telegram (26 Dec 1900), relief keeper Joseph Moore's letter (28 Dec 1900), Superintendent Robert Muirhead's report (8 Jan 1901) and the Northern Lighthouse Board's own summary of the log and slate, plus the Scottish press of 28 Dec 1900 to 12 Jan 1901.
Compiled 2026-10-21.

**Clock note:** Britain kept Greenwich Mean Time in 1900 (no summer time until 1916). Every clock time in the 1900 documents is GMT ("9am", "about noon", "midnight"). Sun and moon times in this file are our own computation and marked [COMPUTED]. US Eastern = GMT minus 5 hours (3:39 p.m. GMT = 10:39 a.m. EST).

**Transcription note:** we did not see the original manuscripts (National Records of Scotland, NLC3/1/1). We read three transcriptions of the same papers: the NLB website (archived 2010 and 2019), the Museum of Scottish Lighthouses website (archived 2007) and the NLB transcripts sent to Mike Dash in 1996 and printed in his 1998 paper. **Each transcription drops a different line.** Where they differ we give both and say which supplies what (section 4.8, section 5).

## How to read this file

Every fact carries a status tag and one or more source IDs (section 0).

- **[CONFIRMED]**: primary record (the 1900-01 telegram, letter, report and press, as transcribed by the NLB, the NRS or Dash) or an official/institutional source (Northern Lighthouse Board, National Records of Scotland, Historic Environment Scotland).
- **[REPORTED]**: reputable secondary history, modern press, family recollection, or a claim by a researcher. Wikipedia is used only as a lead-finder.
- **[DISPUTED]**: sources disagree (both sides given).
- **[LEGEND]**: myth, hoax, fiction or popular error. Do not narrate as fact.
- **[COMPUTED]**: our arithmetic from confirmed inputs (weekdays, day counts, sun and moon, distances, unit conversions). Safe if worded "about".

Rules kept: no invented numbers, dates, quotes or sources. Long quotes come only from 1900-01 primary text. Modern writers (Dash, Bainton, the 1990 Times, Skeptoid, Nicholson, Munro, McCloskey, Love) are paraphrased; any modern quote is under 15 words and attributed.

Content flags for the edit:
- **Women and girls (hard rule): no images of any woman or girl.** That includes James Ducat's widow Mary (née Grierson) and daughters Louisa and Annabella ("Anna"), Donald McArthur's widow, Thomas Marshall's sister, the woman at Crowlista in a 2000 local anecdote, and any woman in the 2018 film *The Vanishing*, *Doctor Who* or other dramatizations. They may appear **in narration only**. Full list in section 18.
- **Real families:** descendants are alive and still comment publicly (a Ducat relative and Joseph Moore's step-grandson both posted on the NRS blog in 2023-2026). Murder and madness are theories with no evidence: label them, keep them short, never present them as the answer.
- **Copyright:** the 1900-01 documents and newspapers are public domain in the US (see section 18 for a UK footnote). Wilfrid Gibson's 1912 poem "Flannan Isle" is **public domain in the US but in copyright in the UK until the end of 2032** (Gibson died 26 May 1962): quote one or two lines at most, or paraphrase. Dash (1998, 2017), the Times (1990), Skeptoid, Bainton and the books cited via Wikipedia are copyrighted: paraphrase. No stills or clips from *The Vanishing* (2018).

### Suggested chapter clock (all defensible)
1. Cold open: about noon, Wednesday 26 Dec 1900. No flag. Whistle, siren, rocket. Moore jumps for the rock.
2. The station: gate closed, doors closed, fire dead "for some days", the clock stopped, beds empty. He runs.
3. Rewind to Thursday 7 Dec 1899: a new light on a 40-acre rock, 20 miles out. The crane lost the first winter.
4. Friday 7 Dec 1900: the last relief. Muirhead shakes their hands. Ducat wishes he could test the signals.
5. Wed 12 Dec: the light seen from Lewis for the last time. Thu 13 Dec: the last log entry.
6. Saturday 15 Dec, 9 a.m.: Ducat chalks the weather on a slate. Forenoon work done; dinner; dishes washed.
7. Afternoon: two men put on sea boots and storm coats. The third leaves his coat on the peg.
8. 3:39 p.m.: sunset. The lamp is never lit. Midnight: the Archtor sees nothing.
9. Eleven dark nights. Thu 20 Dec: the great gale. The relief cannot sail.
10. Back to Boxing Day: the second search, the lightroom in perfect order, the volunteers, "Night coming on". 3:43 p.m.: the light comes back on.
11. The telegram: "A dreadful accident has happened at Flannans."
12. Thursday-Friday: the island searched end to end. West landing: box gone from 110 ft, railings twisted.
13. Saturday 29 Dec: Muirhead on the crane platform, 70 ft up. The life buoy torn off at 110 ft. A stone of more than a ton moved.
14. 8 Jan 1901: "an unexpectedly large roller". The widows, the pensions, the father whose claim was refused.
15. 1901-1965: how the story grew a breakfast, a chair, black birds and a fake log.
16. 1971 to 2026: the light automated, a bronze wave at Breasclete, Moore's letter on display in Edinburgh. Still no bodies, still no witness.

## 8. Theories: what is KNOWN vs what is THEORY

### 8.1 Known (documented)
- The three men were alive and working on the morning of Sat 15 Dec 1900; the day's work and the midday meal were finished. [CONFIRMED]
- Two of them went out dressed for the landings; the third went without his coat. [CONFIRMED]
- The light was not lit that evening and not seen by a passing ship at midnight. [CONFIRMED]
- The sea had recently reached at least 110 ft up the west side and moved a stone of more than a ton. Exactly when (15th or the gale of the 20th) is not known. [CONFIRMED / DISPUTED]
- No bodies were found; no inquiry ever heard evidence in public. [CONFIRMED / REPORTED]

### 8.3 Freak-wave science (context, not proof)
- Rogue waves are real and measured: the Draupner wave, North Sea, 1 Jan 1995, 25.6 m (84 ft) crest to trough, more than twice the local significant wave height (~12 m). [CONFIRMED: DRAUPNER]
- The world-record significant wave height measured by a buoy, 19.0 m (62 ft), was recorded in the North Atlantic between Iceland and the UK on 4 Feb 2013. [CONFIRMED: WMO13]
- A 110-ft run-up on a cliff is not a 110-ft wave: surging water climbs cliffs far above the wave's height. On exposed coasts of Scotland and Ireland, including the Outer Hebrides, storm waves leave boulder deposits on cliff tops "up to 50 m above sea level" (164 ft). [CONFIRMED: HALL06]
- Walter Aldebert (keeper 1953-57) said spray could reach the lamphouse and was nearly washed off at 200 ft (his photos are lost). [REPORTED: DASH-DOCS Doc 10]

## 21. Five candidate cold-open moments (channel hook: 2-second "imagine" or shock line, then weekday + date + clock)

1. **The stopped clock.** "Imagine climbing a cliff to the only house for twenty miles, and finding the clock has stopped." → "Wednesday, December 26, 1900. About noon." Gate shut. Fire cold "for some days". Beds empty. Moore runs.
2. **The coat.** "His coat was still on the peg." → "Wednesday, December 26, 1900. Early afternoon." Two men's sea boots and storm coats gone; McArthur's everyday coat left. Moore: "he went out in shirt sleeves." In December, in the Atlantic.
3. **The dark light.** "Imagine steering for a lighthouse at midnight, and there's nothing there." → "Saturday, December 15, 1900. Midnight." Captain Holman, SS Archtor, out of Philadelphia: a clear, stormy night, no moon yet, no light. He was "satisfied that he should have seen it". His report will not reach the lighthouse board until the keepers have already been found missing.
4. **The slate.** "The last thing they ever wrote was the weather." → "Saturday, December 15, 1900. 9 a.m." Ducat chalks the barometer, the thermometer and the wind. By 3:39 p.m., sunset, the lamp is trimmed, filled, cleaned... and never lit.
5. **The 110-foot sea.** "Imagine the sea reaching 110 feet up a cliff and tearing a life buoy off a railing." → "Saturday, December 29, 1900. Morning." Muirhead on the crane platform, 70 feet up: railings twisted, a stone of more than a ton moved, every rope still coiled. He had shaken their hands three weeks earlier.
