In March nineteen eighty-four, a Canadian flight attendant died in Quebec. He was thirty-one years old.
Three years later, the press gave him a name that would follow him forever.
Patient Zero. The man who brought AIDS to America.
There was just one problem.
That name came from a single letter that someone read wrong.
And the virus had started its journey decades before he was even born, in a forest in central Africa.
Today, about forty-one million people are living with that virus.
This is how it reached them, from the blood of one hunter to every continent on Earth.
And the evidence that cleared Patient Zero was hiding inside the virus itself.
##
June fifth, nineteen eighty-one.
A small weekly bulletin from the Centers for Disease Control printed a short report.
Five young men in Los Angeles, all previously healthy, had come down with a rare kind of pneumonia.
That pneumonia almost only hit people whose immune systems had already collapsed.
Two of the five were dead before the report went out.
A month later, a second report.
Twenty-six men in New York and California, with a rare skin cancer called Kaposi's sarcoma.
Doctors mostly saw that cancer in elderly men.
Now it was showing up in men in their twenties and thirties.
Nobody knew what they were fighting.
All they knew was that their patients' immune systems were falling apart, piece by piece.
In nineteen eighty-two, the disease got its name. Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome. AIDS.
##
To understand why this was so terrifying, you need to know what the virus does inside the body.
Think of your immune system as an army.
And in that army, there is one kind of cell whose job is to lead.
These commanders spot the enemy, and they give the orders for the rest of the army to attack.
This virus goes straight for the commanders.
It gets inside the cell, and turns it into a factory that pumps out new copies of the virus.
The cell dies. And the new copies go looking for the next commander.
Year after year, the army is left without leaders.
And then, germs that a healthy body would beat without you ever noticing, become deadly.
That stage, when the army has lost its commanders, is what we call AIDS.
In nineteen eighty-three, scientists in Paris isolated the virus. It would later be named H I V.
##
But in those years, fear moved faster than science.
People were afraid to shake a patient's hand, or even sit in the same room.
And everyone was asking the same question. Who brought this here?
In nineteen eighty-four, researchers at the CDC published a study of forty men in ten cities.
They found that many of them were linked to each other through sexual contact, like a web.
And close to the middle of that web, was one patient from outside California.
So in their notes, they marked him with the letter O. O for Outside of California.
But on paper, the letter O looks exactly like the number zero.
And little by little, everyone started reading it that way. Patient Zero.
In nineteen eighty-seven, a best-selling book revealed his real name. Gaetan Dugas, a flight attendant for Air Canada.
And a New York tabloid ran the headline: The Man Who Gave Us AIDS.
It was an easy story. One villain. One source.
But the story had a big problem. The timeline didn't fit.
Because, as scientists would later prove, the virus was already in New York before Dugas ever started flying.
So the real question was still open. Where did this virus come from?
##
The answer was waiting in Africa. With chimpanzees.
Monkeys and apes in Africa carry a close cousin of this virus, called S I V.
It has lived inside primates for thousands of years.
So scientists went looking. Which ape carries the version closest to the one in humans?
The problem is, a wild chimpanzee is not going to hold still for a blood test.
So the researchers did something clever. They collected chimpanzee droppings from the forest floor.
Hundreds of samples, from the forests of Cameroon.
And in the southeast corner of Cameroon, they found what they were looking for.
Chimpanzees carrying a virus that was almost a twin of the one that went on to hit the world.
So the virus jumped from ape to human. But how?
The best explanation is hunting.
In these forests, people hunted apes and monkeys for meat.
Picture one hunter, with a cut on his hand, butchering an infected chimpanzee.
The animal's blood gets into the cut. And the virus finds a new host.
This probably happened many times before, and each time the virus died out with its host, in some remote village.
But this time, the virus caught a boat.
##
From southeast Cameroon, rivers flow south, all the way to one city. Kinshasa.
In the nineteen twenties, it was called Leopoldville, and it was one of the fastest growing cities in Africa.
The Belgian colonial government had thousands of men working there, on the docks and on the railway.
Most of them came alone, without their families.
And that created a big market for sex work.
At the same time, colonial health campaigns were treating diseases like syphilis, with injections.
And the needles were often barely sterilized, or not sterilized at all.
One needle, for dozens of people.
A booming city, lots of partners, and dirty needles. It was a perfect setting for a virus.
From Kinshasa, the railway carried it to other cities across the Congo.
By the late nineteen forties, around a million passengers a year were passing through Kinshasa by train.
And nobody noticed. Because this virus is slow.
It can sit in the body for ten years before the person feels anything.
And the people who died from it back then, had other causes written on their death certificates.
##
So how did scientists figure all of this out? How do you trace something that happened a hundred years ago, that nobody ever saw?
The answer is a flaw in the virus itself.
Every time this virus copies itself, it makes mistakes.
Tiny typos in its genetic code.
And every time it passes from one person to the next, those typos pile up.
And they pile up at a fairly steady rate. Like the ticking of a clock.
So if you have two samples of the virus, you can count the differences between them, letter by letter.
And work out how long ago they were the same virus.
But to set that clock, you need very old samples.
And scientists found two.
The first, a blood sample taken from a man in Kinshasa, in nineteen fifty-nine.
The second, a piece of tissue from nineteen sixty, preserved in a block of wax for almost fifty years.
And inside it, there was still a ghost of the virus.
The two samples turned out to be very different from each other. Which meant the virus had already been spreading for a long time.
When scientists ran the clock backwards, they landed on the start of the epidemic. The early nineteen hundreds, in Kinshasa.
In other words, the pandemic had been moving quietly for more than fifty years, before any doctor knew it existed.
##
So how did it cross the ocean?
In nineteen sixty, the Congo won its independence, and it needed teachers and officials who spoke French.
Thousands of people from Haiti went there to work.
And when they went home, it is likely that at least one of them carried the virus back.
From Haiti, the genetic clock says, the virus reached the United States around nineteen seventy.
And in New York, it spread silently, for about ten years.
In twenty sixteen, a team of scientists went back to old blood samples from the late seventies, kept frozen from a study on hepatitis.
And they managed to read the virus's full genetic code.
They also read the virus that Gaetan Dugas had carried.
The result was clear. His virus was an ordinary strain, just like the ones already spreading in America at the time.
He did not bring the virus to America. He caught it, like thousands of others.
Patient Zero was never Patient Zero.
He was just the letter O, read wrong.
##
From one hunter in a forest to today, this virus has infected more than ninety million people.
And about forty-four million have died.
But the story today is very different from the nineteen eighties.
In the nineties, new drugs arrived that stop the virus from copying itself.
They are not a cure. But they put the virus to sleep.
People who take them every day can expect a near normal lifespan.
And when the virus in the blood is so low that a test can't detect it, the person can't pass it on.
There is now even a shot, taken just twice a year, that prevents infection in the first place.
But there are still more than a million new infections every year.
And millions of people don't know they have it.
And the stigma that started with Patient Zero still keeps many people from getting tested.
A virus that traveled for a hundred years, from a forest in Cameroon to every corner of the world.
And the most dangerous thing that helped it along the way, was not the virus itself.
It was that nobody could see it.
