Imagine you're counted at one o'clock. By one-thirty, you're off Alcatraz and into the fog. And nobody will ever find you, dead or alive. How?
Thursday, December sixteenth, nineteen thirty-seven. One in the afternoon. Alcatraz.
The thickest fog of the winter sits on San Francisco Bay. Every prisoner on the island is counted.
At one-thirty, two are gone: Theodore Cole, twenty-five, and Ralph Roe, thirty-one. Both from Oklahoma.
By the end of this file, you'll know how they got out, what the water was doing at that minute, why the warden changed his mind, and why the FBI was still hunting them years later.
##
Before the Battle of Alcatraz, before the raft of nineteen sixty-two, there was this file.
It was only the second escape attempt since the island became a federal prison. The first man was shot dead at the fence.
That same week, a Hollywood movie called Alcatraz Island was playing in theaters across America.
##
Cole and Roe arrived on the island the same day, in October nineteen thirty-five, from Leavenworth.
Ralph Roe: armed robbery of a national bank. Ninety-nine years. His prison card calls him the vicious type, a habitual criminal.
The papers said he once had himself nailed into a box and carried out of the Oklahoma state prison. He gave himself up two hundred yards from the walls. He couldn't breathe.
Theodore Cole: kidnapping. Fifty years. At seventeen, he'd been sentenced to death for a robbery. The sentence was commuted.
His card notes that he escaped from the same Oklahoma prison by hiding in a laundry basket.
A sheriff back home said: Cole was like a greased pig, and I wouldn't be surprised at anything he could do.
##
On Alcatraz, they worked at the north end of the island, in the Model Industries Building, where convicts cut old tires into mats.
Which shop, exactly? The record can't agree. The Bureau of Prisons says the mat shop. The warden said the machine shop. Roe's own prison card says the blacksmith shop.
The warden later said they had sawed through the window bars a few minutes a day, for months, while the noise of the machines covered the sound.
One guard watched their shop. And the shops next to it.
##
Thursday, December sixteenth. One o'clock. The count is complete. Cole and Roe are at work.
Sometime in the next thirty minutes, the last cut goes through.
The gap in the window is about eight and three-quarter inches high and eighteen inches long. They get through it.
Outside, they force the gate of a wire fence with a wrench from the shop, and drop about twenty feet to the rocks.
Up in the towers, armed guards are on watch. The fog is so thick, they can't see them.
Then the two men go into the water.
##
Now, the water. That morning, high tide came at about a quarter past nine. A spring tide: the moon would be full the next morning.
By late morning, the bay had started draining out through the Golden Gate.
By NOAA's modern tide tables, that outgoing current peaked at about a quarter to two, at around four miles an hour past the island, running west, toward the Golden Gate.
And the papers said flood water from inland rivers made the tides that week even higher and more violent than usual.
If they went in during that half hour, they went in at the strongest current of the day.
##
One-thirty. The next count. The warden's words: At one-thirty p.m., they were gone.
He watched driftwood in the current. It went out at high speed, he said, turning over and over.
Coast Guard boats were called by radio. They came within a few yards of the island, and the guards couldn't see them.
Officers searched the caves along the shore. That night, boats crept through the fog at half speed, searchlights on, foghorns blaring.
Soldiers patrolled the shore by the army posts. Police searched the docks.
Ferry pilots said they hadn't seen any strange boats, or anyone swimming.
At four fifty-two, the sun went down on a bay nobody could see across.
##
Friday morning, the fog lifted.
The warden said: The water is too cold, the tide running too high, and land is too far.
Searchers gave them one chance in a thousand. The Coast Guard pulled its cutters back from the search.
Front pages across America: Alcatraz fugitives believed drowned.
Saturday, the FBI's man in San Francisco said: We are going to catch everyone connected with this escape.
And a patrolman in Richmond reported a mystery boat: a launch and a rowboat at an abandoned wharf. Police thought it was illegal fishing.
Then, on Monday, the warden changed his mind.

Now he said the escape wasn't done on the spur of the moment, to use the fog. It had been planned for months. And he believed the two men had been picked up by a boat manned by accomplices.
He had a new idea about the cold water, too. They may have padded themselves with old tire parts from the mat shop before they jumped.
That same Monday, the FBI issued an identification order for Ralph Roe: his fingerprints, his description, his record. Number fourteen seventy-two.
Wednesday, December twenty-second. The warden said the tool that cut the bars was not apparently prison property. A careful check found no such tool missing.
So where did it come from? The record we have never says.
##
Months passed. Men who looked like them were reported all over California.
In August nineteen thirty-nine, the FBI put out a list of the ten most-sought fugitives in the country. Number two: Theodore Cole. Number three: Ralph Roe.
A photo strip ran in the papers: the five toughest men in the United States. One of the other faces was a bank robber named Joseph Cretzer.
Seven years later, Cretzer would be one of the six convicts in the Battle of Alcatraz.
##
Wednesday night, April third, nineteen forty. Tulsa, Oklahoma. A taxi driver is shot in the back. The robbers take three dollars and his cab.
He picks two faces from the newspaper photos: Cole and Roe. Those guys whose pictures were in the papers, he says, shot me.
Federal agents and state police swarm the highways.
The next day, he can't pick them out. The fingerprints from the cab don't match. Police blame two young punks building themselves a reputation.
##
April nineteen forty-one. The San Francisco Chronicle reports that Cole and Roe are alive, in South America.
Its source: prisoners on Alcatraz. The two men had promised to send word if they made it.
A postcard, the paper said, written in July nineteen thirty-eight, carried a code phrase: Business was good in July.
It was never confirmed.
##
No body was ever found. No clothing. Nothing.
The Bureau of Prisons still lists them as missing, and presumed dead.
Their prison cards were never closed. Across each one, stamped in red: Escaped December sixteenth, nineteen thirty-seven.
Twenty-five years later, to the day, another prisoner swam from Alcatraz. He reached the rocks by the Golden Gate Bridge, exhausted, and was caught.
Six months before that, three men had left the same island on a raft. That file is on this channel.
##
So here's what the record shows.
Counted at one. Gone at one-thirty. Into the strongest current of the day, in the thickest fog of the winter. And after that, nothing.
