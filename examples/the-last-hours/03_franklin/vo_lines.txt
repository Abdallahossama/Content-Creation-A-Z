April twenty-fifth, eighteen forty-eight. A frozen shore in the Canadian Arctic.
A man opens a small tin cylinder, and takes out a sheet of paper that was left here eleven months earlier.
On it, in neat handwriting, are two words. All well.
He doesn't start a new page. He starts writing around the edges.
The ships were deserted on the twenty-second, he writes. One hundred and five souls landed here.
Sir John Franklin died on the eleventh of June, eighteen forty-seven.
Then a second hand adds one last line. And start on tomorrow, twenty-sixth, for Back's Fish River.
After that line, nothing.
One hundred and twenty-nine men sailed into the Arctic with Sir John Franklin. Not one of them came home.
This sheet of paper is the only message that tells us what happened to them.
It tells us when they started walking. It doesn't tell us where they stopped.
This is the story of that paper. What it says, what it doesn't, and a door that is still closed at the bottom of the sea.

May nineteenth, eighteen forty-five. Greenhithe, on the River Thames.
Two Royal Navy ships leave England. H.M.S. Erebus, and H.M.S. Terror.
One hundred and thirty-four men on board.
Their mission: find the Northwest Passage. A sea route over the top of North America, from the Atlantic to the Pacific.
Ships had been trying for centuries. Nobody had made it all the way through.
These two carried steam engines, and food for three years.
In command, Sir John Franklin. Fifty-nine years old, and already famous for his earlier Arctic journeys.
Under him, Captain Francis Crozier, of the Terror, and Captain James Fitzjames, of the Erebus.
Before they sailed, the officers sat for portraits made with a camera. Photography was only a few years old.
These are their faces.
##
July, eighteen forty-five. The west coast of Greenland.
The men write their last letters home.
An engineer named John Gregory writes to his wife. It's the last she will ever hear from him.
Five men are too sick to go on. They're sent back to England.
Which means one hundred and twenty-nine sail on.
Remember that number.
##
Late July. Baffin Bay.
Two whaling ships see Erebus and Terror, tied up to an iceberg.
They are the last Europeans who will ever see them.
##
The ships sail west, into the maze of islands at the top of Canada.
North up a channel, as far as latitude seventy-seven. Then back down again.
For their first winter, they stop at a small island called Beechey Island.
On New Year's Day, eighteen forty-six, the first man dies. John Torrington, of the Terror. Twenty years old.
Three days later, John Hartnell, of the Erebus. Twenty-five.
In April, William Braine, a Royal Marine. Thirty-two.
They're buried on the gravel above the beach, under wooden headboards.
You can still read them.
When searchers found these graves, five years later, they found something else.
Nothing. No note. No message about where the ships had gone next.
##
Summer comes. The ice opens. Erebus and Terror sail on, south and west.
September twelfth, eighteen forty-six. Off the northwest coast of King William Island.
The sea freezes around them.
The ships are locked in.
They wait for the ice to let go. It never does.
They will never sail free again.
##
May twenty-fourth, eighteen forty-seven. A Monday.
Lieutenant Graham Gore leaves the ships with another officer, Charles Des Voeux, and six men.
They head across the ice to King William Island, carrying a printed form, sealed inside a tin.
The form was made for bottles thrown into the sea. Whoever finds this, it says in six languages, send it to the Admiralty.
On it, they've written where the ships are, and where they've been.
They even get one thing wrong. It says they spent the winter at Beechey Island in eighteen forty-six. It was eighteen forty-five.
And then, two words. All well.
Gore signs it. Des Voeux signs it. They leave the tin inside a stone cairn.
May twenty-eighth, eighteen forty-seven. All is well.
##
Fourteen days later, Sir John Franklin is dead.
June eleventh, eighteen forty-seven. He was sixty-one.
Nobody wrote down how he died. Or where they put him.
To this day, his grave has never been found.
##
And Franklin isn't the only one.
By the next spring, twenty-four men are dead. Nine officers. Fifteen men.
Graham Gore, the man who signed All well, is one of them.
In the meantime he had been promoted to commander. He didn't live long enough to enjoy it.
The ships have been trapped in the ice for nineteen months.
And their food was only ever meant to last until about July.

April twenty-second, eighteen forty-eight.
One hundred and five men climb down onto the ice, and walk away from Erebus and Terror.
They leave the ships behind, locked in the ice.
Seventeen miles to the shore, hauling boats on heavy sledges.
They come ashore at a place called Victory Point.
A few miles away, Lieutenant John Irving finds Gore's tin from the year before, and they carry it to Victory Point.
Three days later, Captain Fitzjames takes out the paper that says All well.
He doesn't start a new one. He writes in the margins. Around the edges.
The ships were deserted on the twenty-second. Beset since September twelfth, eighteen forty-six.
One hundred and five souls, under the command of Captain Crozier, landed here.
Sir John Franklin died on the eleventh of June. Nine officers and fifteen men are dead.
Crozier signs it. Captain and senior officer.
And then he adds one more line. And start on tomorrow, twenty-sixth, for Back's Fish River.
Years later, the man who found this paper thought that line looked weak and shaky. Like the hand of a sick man.
Back's Fish River is on the mainland, far to the south.
The plan was to reach it, and follow it inland, toward a fur trading post, weeks of travel away.
One hundred and five weak, hungry men. Hauling boats across ice and gravel. On foot.
To lighten the load, they threw away everything they thought they didn't need. Clothes, tools, stoves, pieces of oars.
The tin went back into the stones.
It's the last record ever found that tells us where they were going.
##
Then, silence.
In England, nobody hears a thing. For years.
The Navy sends ships. Then more ships. In the end, more than thirty expeditions go looking for them.
The Admiralty offers twenty thousand pounds to anyone who can save them. A fortune.
In eighteen fifty-four, the Navy gives up. The men are officially declared dead. Their pay stops.
That same year, an explorer named John Rae is surveying the Arctic coast for the Hudson's Bay Company.
And Inuit hunters tell him a story.
A few winters before, they say, about forty white men were seen walking south over the ice, dragging a boat.
They were thin. They bought a seal from the hunters.
Later that season, the Inuit found bodies. About thirty of them, on the mainland, near the mouth of a great river.
And they told Rae something else.
That the last of them, starving, had been driven to eat the dead.
In England, people refused to believe it. Charles Dickens wrote that it couldn't be true.
But Rae had been told the truth. And it would be proven.
##
Franklin's wife never stopped looking. She bought a small ship called the Fox, and sent it north.
Eighteen fifty-nine. Lieutenant William Hobson, of the Fox, is crossing King William Island by sledge.
May sixth. At a quarter past eleven in the morning, his party stops beside a large cairn, and pitches a tent.
Among the loose stones that have fallen from the cairn, there's a small tin cylinder.
Inside, the paper. All well. And the margins.
It had been lying there for eleven years.
Around the cairn, piles of clothing, tools, stoves, cooking pots. Everything the men had thrown away.
The captain of the Fox, Francis McClintock, read it later. He wrote, a sad tale was never told in fewer words.
##
May twenty-fourth, eighteen fifty-nine. Just after midnight, on the south coast of the island.
McClintock finds a skeleton, lying face down on a ridge of gravel.
A young man, dressed like a ship's steward. In his pocket, a little book full of papers.
The papers belong to a sailor named Harry Peglar.
For a hundred and sixty-seven years, people argued about it. Was this Peglar? Or a steward, carrying another man's papers?
This year, DNA finally answered. It was Peglar.
##
That same afternoon, about three o'clock, on the west coast, one of Hobson's men spots something on the beach.
A boat. Twenty-eight feet long, sitting on a heavy wooden sledge.
Boat and sledge together weigh about fourteen hundred pounds. Dragged by men who were starving.
Inside, two skeletons.
Two guns, standing upright against the side. One barrel of each, still loaded.
And the strangest cargo. Silver forks and spoons, some with Franklin's crest. Five watches. A novel. Soap. A toothbrush.
Nearly forty pounds of chocolate. And no meat. No bread.
And the boat was pointing northeast. Back toward the ships.
Were they turning back? McClintock thought so. Nobody knows.
##
The Inuit had been telling the searchers what happened all along.
In eighteen fifty-nine, an old Inuk told McClintock's interpreter that the white men fell down and died as they walked along.
Some were buried. Some were not.
Others said one ship was crushed by the ice and sank. The other was forced ashore, further south.
##
So what killed them?
For decades, the favorite answer was lead. Lead from the solder in their tin cans.
In the nineteen eighties, scientists found high levels of lead in the men buried on Beechey Island.
But newer studies say lead probably wasn't the main killer.
Most researchers now see no single cause. Hunger. Cold. Disease. Scurvy. All at once.
And in nineteen ninety-seven, a study of bones at a site on Erebus Bay found cut marks on about a quarter of them.
John Rae had been right.
##
For more than a hundred and fifty years, nobody could find the ships.
Then, September second, twenty fourteen.
A Parks Canada archaeologist named Ryan Harris is watching a sonar screen.
And a ship appears. Intact. In just thirty-six feet of water.
It's H.M.S. Erebus.
They found it in the area where Inuit accounts had said a ship went down.
Two years later, an Inuk hunter named Sammy Kogvik told a research crew about a mast he'd once seen, sticking out of the ice in a bay on King William Island.
September, twenty sixteen. They went to look. And found H.M.S. Terror.
In twenty nineteen, a remote camera went inside.
Plates and glasses, still on the shelves. Beds. Desks.
And on the lower deck, there is one door that's still closed.
The captain's sleeping cabin.
Parks Canada thinks there could still be papers in there.
##
On land, the bones of the men who walked have been studied for decades.
And since twenty twenty-one, DNA from living relatives has started to give some of them their names back.
John Gregory, the engineer who wrote that last letter to his wife.
Captain James Fitzjames. The man who wrote in the margins of the note.
His remains were found at Erebus Bay. And his bones carry the same cut marks.
In the end, rank didn't save anyone.
This year, four more. William Orren, an able seaman. David Young, a boy of the ship's crew. John Bridgens, a steward. And Harry Peglar.
Six men, out of one hundred and twenty-nine, have their names back.
One hundred and twenty-three still don't.
##
So here's what the records can tell you, and what they can't.
They can tell you that one hundred and twenty-nine men sailed into the ice.
That on May twenty-eighth, eighteen forty-seven, all was well.
That eleven months later, twenty-four were dead, and one hundred and five started walking south.
They can't tell you where Franklin is buried.
They can't tell you who was the last man alive, or how far he got.
Some Inuit accounts talk of footprints on the mainland, years later. First four men, then three. None of it has ever been proven.
And they can't tell you what's behind that closed door.
They told us when they started walking. Not where they stopped.
