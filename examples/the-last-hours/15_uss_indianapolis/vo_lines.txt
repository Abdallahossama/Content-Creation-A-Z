Imagine you're in the sea for four days. Eight American planes fly over you. Men are dying around you. And nobody has even reported your ship missing. Why?
Monday, July thirtieth, nineteen forty-five. About five minutes past midnight. The Philippine Sea.
The heavy cruiser Indianapolis is hit by two torpedoes. About twelve minutes later, she's gone.
Eleven hundred and ninety-five men were aboard. Three hundred and sixteen will come home.
By the end of this file, you'll know what she had just delivered, how one sentence about paperwork kept her missing for four days, what the sharks really did, and what the man who sank her told an American court.
##
Two weeks earlier. Sunday, July fifteenth. Hunters Point, San Francisco.
A large crate is lashed down in the ship's hangar. A canister is welded to the deck of a cabin. Two Army officers come aboard to ride with it.
Monday, at about eight in the morning, she sails. A few hours earlier, in the New Mexico desert, the first atomic bomb had been tested.
She runs from the Farallon Islands to Diamond Head in seventy-four and a half hours. A record.
On Thursday, July twenty-sixth, at Tinian, the cargo goes ashore: parts of the first atomic bomb ever used in war, including its uranium.
Remember that cargo. It comes back at the worst possible moment.
##
Saturday, July twenty-eighth. Nine in the morning. She sails from Guam for Leyte, in the Philippines. Alone. No escort.
Her routing orders list three recent submarine reports along the way. And they say: Zigzag at discretion of the Commanding Officer.
Remember that sentence. Four months later, it puts her captain on trial.
What Captain Charles McVay isn't told: American codebreakers have read that a Japanese submarine, I-58, is heading out on patrol. Ship captains never saw secrets like that.
Sunday afternoon, she passes a landing ship, LST-779. It's the last American ship that will ever see her.
That ship's log will matter again, seventy-one years later.
##
After dark, she stops zigzagging. The sky is cloudy. McVay would later testify that the visibility was poor.
At about ten thirty, the moon rises, three-quarters full.
She's making seventeen knots on a straight course. McVay is asleep in his cabin off the bridge.
Just after eleven thirty, the submarine I-58 comes up to the surface. Her captain, Commander Mochitsura Hashimoto, sees a dark shape under the moon, about six miles off, coming straight at him.
He dives. For twenty-seven minutes, he lines up his shot.
He also carries manned suicide torpedoes. He doesn't use them. Ordinary torpedoes, he decides, will be enough.
From about sixteen hundred yards, he fires six, three seconds apart.
##
Monday, July thirtieth. About five past midnight, ship's time. Two of them hit the starboard bow.
In America, it's still Sunday morning.
A blaze shoots through the front half of the ship. The phones go dead. Nobody on the bridge can stop the engines.
McVay sends his navigator to the radio room with a message: hit by two torpedoes, sinking rapidly, need immediate assistance.
A radioman keys S.O.S. again and again. It most likely never left the ship.
The executive officer reports: we are definitely going down. McVay answers: Pass the word to abandon ship. With the phones dead, it goes by word of mouth.
She rolls onto her side and hangs there for about two minutes. McVay is pulled off her side into the sea, her propellers turning above him.
About twelve minutes after the hit, she goes down by the bow.
Roughly three hundred men go down with her. Around eight hundred are in the sea, most with only a life jacket.
##
On the submarine, Hashimoto hears explosions, surfaces, and sees nothing. He radios that he has sunk something.
Within hours, American codebreakers read that report: attacked and sank one, and then a blank. Japanese claims were so often false that it was given a low value.
Tuesday, July thirty-first. Eleven in the morning. She's due at Leyte.
She doesn't arrive. And nobody reports it.
A fleet rule said: Arrival reports shall not be made for combatant ships. At Leyte, it was read to mean: no report when they don't arrive, either.
The officer who knows she hasn't come in takes no action. A radio test that day can't reach her. Nobody asks why.
On the plotting boards at Guam and Leyte, she's shown as having arrived.
##
Out on the water, the survivors are strung out over about twenty miles, in groups that can't see each other.
McVay's group: three rafts, a floater net, nine men. Some Spam, malted milk tablets, and a flare pistol.
The biggest group, about three hundred men, has no rafts, no food and no water. Just life jackets.
The kapok jackets were only meant to hold a man up for about sixty-four hours. After two days in the water, they ride so low that if a man's head falls forward, he can drown.
Many were badly burned when the torpedoes hit. Very few of the badly hurt survived the water.
Days of sun. No fresh water. Some men drink the sea. Some see things that aren't there: the ship, just under the surface, with fresh milk aboard. Some swim off and are never seen again.
And over their heads, planes fly their regular patrols. The survivors count about eight. The planes are watching their radar for submarines. Nobody looks down.
##
Now, the sharks. Thirty years later, a movie made this story famous.
In Jaws, a character tells it this way: eleven hundred men went into the water, three hundred and sixteen came out, and the sharks took the rest.
The record is different. Around eight hundred went into the water. Sharks were there from early on, and they were bothering men who were alone.
But the ship's doctor, Lewis Haynes, said that in a hundred and ten hours in the water, he never saw a man attacked by a shark.
McVay's own report lists the causes of death: wounds, exhaustion, thirst, drinking salt water. Sharks are on that list too. How many men they killed, no record can tell us.

Thursday, August second. Peleliu. At ten past nine, Lieutenant Wilbur Gwinn takes off in a Ventura bomber on a routine patrol.
The sea is like glass. Nobody has told him a ship is missing. Because nobody knows.
At about eleven, his trailing radio antenna breaks. He walks back to the tunnel at the tail of the plane to fix it. And he looks down.

He's right over an oil slick. He follows it down to nine hundred feet.
At eleven eighteen, he sees heads in the water.
At eleven twenty-five, he radios: Sighted thirty survivors. Dropped transmitter and life boat.
Then he keeps counting. A hundred and fifty.
Nobody had been looking for them. They were found because an antenna broke.
That same day, a message finally goes out to Guam: Indianapolis has not arrived Leyte. Advise.
##
In the afternoon, Lieutenant Adrian Marks arrives in a PBY flying boat. He sees men alone in the water, and sharks bothering them. No ship can get there before midnight.
At five-oh-five, he lands on the open sea, in twelve-foot swells. In his words: the plane landed in three bounces, the first bounce being about fifteen feet high.
Rivets pop. His crew plugs the holes with pencils and cotton.
He picks up the men who are alone first. When the hull is full, he lays them on the wings. Fifty-six men.
Their first request, he said, was for water.
At ten forty-two that night, the destroyer escort Cecil J. Doyle turns her searchlight straight up into the sky, in waters where a submarine had sunk a cruiser. There is no moon.
All night and into Friday, ships pull men out of the sea.
McVay's rafts are miles to the north. He watches the planes work away from him. Then a ship finds them by radar, off an empty ammunition can. We were never sighted by a plane, he said.
By noon on Friday, August third, every survivor is out of the water. Almost four and a half days.
##
Of eleven hundred and ninety-five men, three hundred and sixteen came home.
For seventy-three years, the Navy's count said eleven hundred and ninety-six. In twenty eighteen, historians found a sailor on the list who had walked off before she sailed.
Monday, August sixth. Her survivors are in hospital beds on Peleliu and Samar. The cargo she delivered is dropped on Hiroshima.
##
Now, remember that sentence: Zigzag at discretion of the Commanding Officer.
Monday, December third, nineteen forty-five. The Washington Navy Yard. Captain McVay goes on trial. Charge one: hazarding his ship by failing to zigzag.
Thursday, December thirteenth. The prosecution calls a witness who has never been to the United States: Commander Hashimoto, the man who sank her.
The defense asks him: would zigzagging have made any difference?
His answer, through an interpreter: It would have involved no change in the method of firing the torpedoes, but some changes in maneuvering.
An American submarine captain told the court a zigzag would not have saved her.
Wednesday, December nineteenth. The court finds McVay not guilty of being late to abandon ship, and guilty of failing to zigzag. He was never convicted of losing his ship.
The court asked for clemency. Two months later, the Secretary of the Navy cancelled the sentence and restored him to duty.
##
In November nineteen sixty-eight, Charles McVay took his own life.
In nineteen ninety-six, an eleven-year-old in Pensacola, Florida, asked his father whether the story in Jaws was true.
For a school history project, he sent questionnaires to a hundred and fifty-four survivors. More than eighty wrote back. Three years later, he testified before the Senate.
In two thousand, Congress said McVay's record should show that he is exonerated for the loss of the Indianapolis and so many of her crew. In twenty-oh-one, the Navy put those words in his file. The conviction itself still stands.
In August twenty seventeen, her wreck was found, more than eighteen thousand feet down, west of where the Navy had placed her. That landing ship's log had moved the search.

Two torpedoes sank her in about twelve minutes. A rule about paperwork kept her missing for four days. And a broken antenna found her.
