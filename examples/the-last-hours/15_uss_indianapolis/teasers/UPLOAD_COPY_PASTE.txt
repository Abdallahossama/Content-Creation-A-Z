Two Torpedoes. 12 Minutes. Then the USS Indianapolis Was Gone. #ussindianapolis

Monday, July 30, 1945, about five past midnight. Two torpedoes from the Japanese submarine I-58 hit the starboard bow. The phones go dead and nobody on the bridge can stop the engines. A radioman keys S.O.S. again and again; it most likely never left the ship. About twelve minutes after the hit, she goes down by the bow.

His Antenna Broke. That's How They Found the USS Indianapolis Survivors. #ussindianapolis

Thursday, August 2, 1945. A Navy patrol pilot goes aft to fix a broken antenna, looks down, and sees an oil slick. He follows it down to nine hundred feet. At 11:25 he radios: "Sighted thirty survivors." Then he keeps counting. Nobody had been looking for them. They were found because an antenna broke.

The USS Indianapolis Never Arrived. Nobody Reported It. #ussindianapolis

Tuesday, July 31, 1945, 11 a.m. The USS Indianapolis is due at Leyte. She doesn't arrive, and nobody reports it. A fleet rule said arrival reports shall not be made for combatant ships; at Leyte it was read to mean no report when they don't arrive, either. On the plotting boards she's shown as having arrived.

The Man Who Sank the USS Indianapolis Testified at Her Captain's Trial #ussindianapolis

Thursday, December 13, 1945. At Captain McVay's court-martial, the prosecution calls Commander Mochitsura Hashimoto, the man who sank his ship. Would zigzagging have made a difference? His answer: no change in the method of firing the torpedoes, only some changes in maneuvering. McVay was found guilty of failing to zigzag.
