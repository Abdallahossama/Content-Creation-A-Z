In 1587, about 115 English men, women and children were left on Roanoke Island, off what is now North Carolina. Their governor, John White, sailed home for supplies. He made a plan with them: if they moved, carve the name of the place. If they were in danger, carve a cross. It took him three years to come back.

This is his return, hour by hour, in his own words, from the 1600 printing of his account: the smoke on the island, the boat that rolled over at the inlet and the seven men who drowned, the trumpet and the English songs played into the dark with no answer, the footprints at daybreak, the letters C R O on a tree, the word CROATOAN on a gatepost, and no cross. Then the storm, the lost anchors, and the theories, including the two rival claims of 2026.
