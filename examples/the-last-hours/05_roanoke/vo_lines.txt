Imagine you wait three years, and lose seven men, to reach your family.
But nobody is there. Just one word, carved on a post.
Tuesday, August eighteenth, fifteen ninety. About five in the morning.
Roanoke Island, off the coast of what is now North Carolina. Nineteen men climb out of two boats onto the sand.
The day before, seven of their crew drowned trying to get here.
In the dark, they had sounded a trumpet and played English songs toward a fire in these woods, and called out in a friendly way.
John White wrote down what came back. But we had no answer.
White is the governor of a colony he left on this island. More than a hundred men, women and children. His daughter. His granddaughter.
In the next few hours, he will find a word carved into a post. And he will look for one more mark, a cross.
What he found that morning is still the best clue anyone has.

This is The Last Hours.
##
Three years earlier. Roanoke Island, the summer of fifteen eighty-seven.
About a hundred and fifteen colonists have just landed. Men, women and children. The first English settlement in America with families in it.
They weren't supposed to be here. The plan was the Chesapeake Bay, farther north. The ships' pilot, Simon Fernandes, wouldn't take them any farther.
And this island had a history.
A year before, English soldiers had killed Wingina, the leader of the Roanoke people, and cut off his head.
So the colony was planted next to neighbors with every reason to hate them.
Within a week, one of the colonists, George Howe, was killed while catching crabs alone.
Their real friends lived about fifty miles south, on an island called Croatoan.
It was the home of Manteo, a Croatoan man who had been to England twice, and came back as their guide.
On August eighteenth, the governor's daughter, Eleanor Dare, gave birth to a girl. They named her Virginia. The first English child born in the Americas.
The governor was her grandfather. His name was John White. He was an artist. Two years earlier, he had painted this coast, its people and its towns.
Four days after the birth, the colonists came to him, with one voice, and asked him to sail back to England for supplies.
He refused. They kept asking. Finally, he agreed.
Before he left, they made a plan. They might move, maybe fifty miles inland.
If they did, they would carve the name of the place they had gone to, on a tree or a doorpost.
And if they were in danger, they would carve a cross above it.
On the morning of August twenty-seventh, fifteen eighty-seven, John White sailed away. His granddaughter was nine days old.
##
Then the world got in the way.
In fifteen eighty-eight, the Spanish Armada sailed for England, and ships were held at home.
White tried anyway, with two tiny ships. A French ship boarded his and robbed it. He was wounded, and had to turn back.
In fifteen ninety, he finally got a ride, on a fleet of privateers hunting Spanish ships.
They let him bring nothing. In his words, not so much as a boy to serve him.
The privateers spent most of the summer raiding in the Caribbean. Roanoke came last.
On August twelfth, his ships anchored for a night off the north end of Croatoan. He had no idea yet that it mattered.
##
Saturday, August fifteenth. Toward evening.
The ships anchor off the Outer Banks, about ten miles from shore. And White sees smoke.
A great smoke, rising from Roanoke Island, near the place where he had left the colony.
He wrote that it put them in good hope. Someone was there. Someone was waiting.
##
Sunday morning, the sixteenth. They fire cannon from the ships, one shot at a time, so the colonists will hear them.
Then they row for shore. On the way, they see a second smoke, farther south, and go to that one first.
It is much farther than it looks. They walk until they are very sore and tired. No people. No fresh water.
That night, they are back on the ships. Roanoke will have to wait one more day.
##
Monday, August seventeenth. Ten in the morning.
Two boats push off for Roanoke. A gale is blowing from the northeast, straight into the inlet.
The sea is breaking hard on the sandbar. White's boat makes it across, half full of water.
The second boat, Captain Edward Spicer's, is halfway over the bar when a wave flips it.
It rolls over, keel up, two or three times. The men cling to it.
Spicer and his mate, White wrote, hung on until they sank, and were seen no more.
Eleven men were in that boat. Seven drowned.
Spicer was the captain who, three years earlier, had brought the colony's second ship safely to Roanoke.
Now the sailors want to quit. All of them, White wrote, of one mind. Go no farther.
White and Captain Cooke talk them out of it.
##
Late in the day, they set out again. Nineteen men in two boats.
It is so dark, they row a quarter of a mile past the settlement without seeing it.
Then, at the north end of the island, a fire, burning through the woods.
They drop anchor near the shore. The trumpet. The English songs. The friendly calls.
No answer.
They wait for daylight.

Tuesday, August eighteenth, fifteen ninety. Daybreak.
It is exactly three years since his granddaughter was born. White never mentions it.
They land and walk to the fire. It is just grass and rotten trees, burning. Nobody.
But in the sand, there are footprints. Two or three different people. Made during the night.
They walk around the north end of the island. At the top of a sandy bank, there is a tree.
Carved into it, in clear Roman letters, are three: C. R. O.
White knew what that meant. It was the code.
They walk on to where he had left the colony. The houses are gone. Taken down, not burned.
In their place stands a palisade, a wall of tall tree trunks, built like a fort. It was not there when he left.
On a post at the right side of the entrance, the bark has been peeled away.
Five feet from the ground, in capital letters, someone carved one word. Croatoan.
He looked for the other sign. The cross. The one they had agreed would mean danger.
There was no cross.
Inside the walls, bars of iron, lumps of lead and small guns lie scattered on the ground, almost overgrown with grass and weeds.
The boats are gone. The small cannon left with the colony are gone.
Then the sailors find chests that had been buried, and dug up again, and broken open. Three of them belong to White.
His books, torn from their covers. His pictures and maps, rotting in the rain. His armor, almost eaten through with rust.
He blamed their enemies across the water, at the Roanoke town on the mainland.
And still, he wrote that he was overjoyed.
Croatoan was Manteo's home. Its people were friends. No cross meant no danger. His family was safe, fifty miles south.
All he had to do was sail there.
##
Later that day, the sky clouds over. It looks like a foul and stormy night is coming.
They hurry back to the boats, and reach the ships that evening, with much danger and labor.
Six men filling water casks on a little island have to be fetched in the dark. The full casks are left on the beach.
That night is stormy and foul.
##
Wednesday, August nineteenth. Morning. The wind is good for Croatoan.
They start to raise the anchor. The cable snaps.
They lose a second anchor. A third drags across the bottom, and the ship nearly runs aground.
White wrote it down. One cable and anchor left, out of four.
Food is short. The fresh water is lost.
So the captain makes a new plan. Sail south, spend the winter in the Caribbean, and come back on the way home.
That same night, the ships part. White's ship heads south.
Nine days later, a storm out of the west drives them out into the Atlantic, toward the Azores. And then home.
On October twenty-fourth, fifteen ninety, John White anchored at Plymouth, England.
He never saw America again.
##
In fifteen ninety-three, he wrote a letter from his house in Ireland.
He said he was giving up. And he left the colonists to the merciful help of the Almighty.
Nobody knows when or where John White died.
##
For years, no one really searched.
In sixteen oh seven, the English founded Jamestown, about a hundred miles to the north. And then, the stories started.
Native people told John Smith about men inland, dressed like him.
A Jamestown writer, William Strachey, claimed the colonists had lived peacefully among Native people for twenty years, until the great chief Powhatan had them killed.
He also wrote that seven had been kept alive by another chief, to beat copper.
Historians treat all of it as hearsay. Nobody has ever found proof.
More than a century after White's visit, an English explorer, John Lawson, met the Hatteras people, who lived on the old island of Croatoan.
They told him some of their ancestors were white people, who could read from a book. And he noticed gray eyes among them.
##
There is one more witness, and it is made of wood.
In the late nineteen nineties, scientists read the rings of ancient cypress trees from this coast.
The years fifteen eighty-seven to fifteen eighty-nine were the worst drought in eight hundred years.
The colony arrived at the start of it, beside Native towns that were already short of corn.
##
Today there are two main theories, and both have teams digging.
One says they went where the post said. Croatoan, now called Hatteras Island.
Archaeologists there have found English objects from the right era. Part of a sword, a slate, gun parts, and waste from iron working.
Skeptics say all of it could have arrived through trade.
The other theory says they did what they told White they might. They moved about fifty miles inland, up the Chowan River.
In twenty twelve, researchers found a patch glued onto one of John White's own maps. Underneath was a hidden symbol, like a fort, at the head of that river.
Digs nearby have turned up English pottery from the right period.
And this year, both sides went further.
In June, the mainland team announced, in their words, that the Lost Colony is found. Settled on farms along the Chowan River.
That same month, the Hatteras team said deer teeth from their site, with a musket ball in the jaw, dated to the fifteen eighties.
Neither claim has been peer reviewed. And the National Park Service still says no one has proved what happened.
##
So here is what the records can tell you, and what they can't.
They can tell you that by August fifteen ninety, the colony was gone, and had left in an orderly way.
Houses taken down. A wall built. Chests buried. Boats taken.
They can tell you the colonists left a message, Croatoan, and no sign of danger.
They can't tell you if all of them went there, or only some.
They can't tell you who lit the fire John White saw, or whose footprints were in the sand that morning.
They can't tell you why the letters on the tree stop at C. R. O.
And they can't tell you what happened to a three-year-old girl named Virginia.
On the morning of August eighteenth, fifteen ninety, John White read one word on a gatepost.
Four hundred and thirty-six years later, it is still the best clue anyone has.
