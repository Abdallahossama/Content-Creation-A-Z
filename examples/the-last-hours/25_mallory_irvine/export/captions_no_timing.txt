Imagine standing on the roof of the world, nearly thirty years before anyone officially got there.
Sunday, June eighth, nineteen twenty-four. Twelve fifty in the afternoon.
On the north side of Mount Everest, a geologist named Noel Odell looks up through a gap in the clouds.
High on the summit ridge, he sees two small black dots, moving up the snow.
George Mallory, and Andrew Irvine.
Then the clouds close in.
And nobody ever sees them alive again.
Did they reach the top of the world, and die on the way down?
Or did they turn back, and fall short?
Seventy-five years later, a team of climbers found one of them.
And what was in his pockets, still hasn't settled the question.
In the nineteen twenties, Mount Everest was the last great prize left on Earth.
The North Pole and the South Pole had been reached. The highest mountain had not.
And no one wanted it more than George Mallory.
He was thirty-seven, a schoolteacher from England, and one of the best climbers of his time.
This was his third expedition to Everest, in four years.
When a reporter in New York asked him why he wanted to climb it, he gave an answer that became famous.
Because it's there.
His partner was the youngest man on the team.
Andrew Irvine. Twenty-two years old. A student at Oxford, and a gifted engineer.
Irvine had almost no experience on big mountains.
But he could do something nobody else on the team could.
He could fix the oxygen sets.
The nineteen twenty-four British expedition walked for weeks across Tibet to reach the mountain.
Then storms, cold and sickness wore the team down, camp by camp.
On June fourth, a climber named Edward Norton pushed higher than any human had ever been.
Twenty-eight thousand, one hundred and twenty-six feet. Without bottled oxygen.
He turned back, less than a thousand feet below the summit.
The expedition was nearly out of time. The monsoon was coming.
Mallory decided on one last try. With oxygen. And he chose Irvine to go with him.
June sixth. Mallory and Irvine leave the camp on the North Col.
Before they go, another climber takes their photograph. It is the last picture of them together.
June seventh. They reach the highest camp, a tiny tent clinging to the slope, at about twenty-six thousand eight hundred feet.
That evening, Mallory sends a note down with the porters.
It says perfect weather for the job.
He tells the expedition photographer to watch for them on the ridge, the next morning, around eight o'clock.
June eighth. They set off from the high camp.
They carry oxygen tanks on their backs, about thirty pounds each.
And they are believed to be carrying a small Kodak camera.
If they reach the top, that camera could prove it.
Odell is climbing up behind them, alone, in support.
The mountain is wrapped in mist.
Then, at twelve fifty, the mist clears for a moment.
Odell later wrote that he saw a tiny black spot moving up a snow slope toward a rock step on the ridge. Then a second spot joined it.
They were going strong for the top.
But by Mallory's own plan, they were hours behind schedule.
And then, the clouds closed.
Around two in the afternoon, a snow squall sweeps the upper mountain.
Odell climbs on to the high camp, and waits.
He whistles and shouts into the wind, in case they are lost nearby.
Nothing.
As evening comes, he goes back down to the North Col, expecting them to follow.
They never do.
Two days later, Odell climbs all the way back up to the high tent. It is exactly as he left it.
To tell the men waiting below, he drags two sleeping bags onto the snow, and lays them out in the shape of a T.
The agreed signal for: no trace.
For nine years, the mountain kept its secret.
Then in nineteen thirty-three, another British team found something on the ridge.
An ice axe, lying on the bare rock, at about twenty-seven thousand seven hundred feet.
It had belonged to Andrew Irvine.
It was below the place where Odell had seen them. Was it dropped in a fall? Or just set down on the way up?
Nobody could say.
Then, in nineteen seventy-five, a Chinese climber named Wang Hongbao came across a body in the snow, high on the north face.
He said it was an English dead, in old fashioned clothes.
He told a Japanese climber about it, in nineteen seventy-nine.
The very next day, Wang was killed in an avalanche, before he could show anyone where.
May first, nineteen ninety-nine.
A search team climbs onto the north face, looking for the spot that Wang described.
An American climber named Conrad Anker sees something white, on the slope below him.
It is a body, face down, frozen into the scree.
His clothes still have a name tag sewn in.
G. Mallory.
He had been lying there for seventy-five years.
A broken rope was tied around his waist. That means the two men were roped together when something went wrong.
His right leg was badly broken. And there was a wound on his forehead.
He had fallen. But he had survived the fall long enough to come to a stop, with his good leg crossed over the broken one.
Then the team checked his pockets.
They found letters, a pocket knife, an altimeter, a pair of snow goggles, and his notes on the oxygen tanks.
Three clues have kept people arguing ever since.
First, the goggles were in his pocket.
On a bright day at that height, you wear them, or you go snow blind.
Goggles in the pocket suggests he was moving when the light was gone. After sunset. Which means they were still up there very, very late.
Second, Mallory had told his family he would leave a photograph at the summit.
That photograph was not on his body.
Third, the camera.
The Kodak was not with him. If Irvine was carrying it, the film inside may still show what they saw that day.
Experts say film that old, frozen that long, might still be developed.
So did they make it?
Between them and the summit stood a wall of rock on the ridge, now called the Second Step. About a hundred feet high.
In nineteen seventy-five, Chinese climbers bolted an aluminium ladder to it.
Most climbers since have used that ladder.
In two thousand seven, Conrad Anker, the man who found Mallory, removed the ladder for a climb, and went up the Second Step free, to test it.
He made it. But he said it would have been very hard for Mallory, in nineteen twenty-four gear.
Many experts think the Second Step stopped them.
Others point to the goggles, the missing photograph, and the late hour, and believe they went higher than anyone thinks.
The first confirmed climb to the top of Everest came in May, nineteen fifty-three.
Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay.
Twenty-nine years after Mallory and Irvine vanished into the cloud.
Andrew Irvine has never been found.
Neither has the camera.
Somewhere on the north face, there may be a small Kodak, with a roll of film inside.
And on that film, the answer to the oldest question in mountaineering.
What happened on that ridge, after twelve fifty?
