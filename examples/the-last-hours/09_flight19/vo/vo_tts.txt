Imagine you're one of fourteen men lost off Florida. By six P M, the Navy knows roughly where you are. But nobody ever tells you.
Wednesday, December fifth, nineteen forty-five. Seven oh four in the evening.
At a Navy air station in Fort Lauderdale, one line goes into the crash log.
Last transmissions from the flight. Tee three calling Tee twenty-eight. No answer.
Five Navy bombers. Fourteen men. In eighty years, not one piece of them has been found.
This is Flight Nineteen, the story the Bermuda Triangle legend grew out of.
Forget the legend. That afternoon, Navy radio operators typed out what they could hear of these men, line by line.
By the end, you'll know where they really were, what the Navy knew by six that night, and which famous last words were made up seventeen years later.
##
Eleven that morning, the base weather office issues its forecast. Showers. A cold front late in the afternoon. Flying: average to undesirable.
You'll hear it was a calm, perfect day. The Navy's own forecast says it wasn't.
Higher up, the wind is blowing thirty to fifty knots from the west-southwest. Flying out, it pushes you along. Flying home, it's in your face.
Remember the wind.
The flight leader is Lieutenant Charles Taylor, twenty-eight. About twenty-five hundred hours in the air, and a combat tour from the carrier Hancock.
He's been at this base for two weeks. It's his first time leading this route.
Behind him fly four students with about three hundred hours each: three Marine officers and a Navy ensign. And nine radiomen and gunners, teenagers and men in their twenties.
It's not a patrol. It's a training flight called Navigation Problem Number One. A triangle over the Bahamas, about three hundred sixty miles, back by five twenty-three.
East to a shoal for bombing practice. On east, then north across Grand Bahama, then home.
The tanks are full and the life rafts are packed. But none of the five planes has its cockpit clock. The men are expected to wear their wristwatches.
Remember the clocks.
Two ten in the afternoon. Five Avenger torpedo bombers take off from Fort Lauderdale. A student leads. Taylor flies behind him, watching.
##
By about three, the practice bombs are dropped, and the flight heads on east.
Somewhere on the second leg, Taylor decides the students are going the wrong way. He takes over the lead.
Three forty. Near the base, another instructor, Lieutenant Robert Cox, is gathering his own students in the air.
On the radio, he hears a man calling someone named Powers, asking for his compass reading. Again and again.
Then: I don't know where we are. We must have got lost after that last turn.
Cox calls back: whoever is calling Powers, identify yourself.
Around four, the answer comes from Taylor, as Cox later told the Navy.
Both my compasses are out. I'm sure I'm in the Keys, but I don't know how far down, and I don't know how to get to Fort Lauderdale.
The Florida Keys are southwest of Fort Lauderdale. Taylor is over the Bahamas, on the other side of Florida.
Cox tells him: put the sun on your port wing and fly up the coast. What's your altitude? I'll fly south and meet you.
The reply: I know where I am now. I'm at twenty-three hundred feet. Don't come after me.
Cox: I'm coming to meet you anyhow.
That sentence, Don't come after me, is real. It's in Cox's sworn testimony. In nineteen seventy-four, a bestseller added a few words to it. We'll get to those.
Cox flies south. Taylor's voice gets weaker and weaker. Then Cox's own transmitter fails.
Later, Cox will say Taylor must have been heading north as he headed south.
##
Four twenty-one. A rescue radio station at Port Everglades picks Taylor up.
Am lost. At present time I am over small islands believed to be the Florida Keys. My compass is out.
Four twenty-five. We have just passed over small island. We have no other land in sight.
Four twenty-six. Does anyone in the area have a radar screen that could pick us up?
Four twenty-eight. The station suggests a plane with a good compass take over the lead. As far as the station could tell, no other plane ever did.
Four thirty-one. Taylor passes on what one of his students thinks.
If we went two seventy, we could hit land.
Two seventy is due west. And west is Florida. Remember that student.
So why don't they turn west? Four forty-five.
We are heading zero three zero for forty-five minutes. Then we will fly north to make sure we are not over the Gulf of Mexico.
The Gulf is on the far side of Florida. Taylor is starting to think he might be there.
The Navy's board would later write that he was uncertain as to in what direction lay the peninsula of Florida.
##
On the ground, the alarm spreads. At four forty-seven, direction-finding stations along the coast are told to take bearings on the flight's radio.
Port Everglades asks for the base's ready plane, a standby aircraft, to fly out east and find them.
Remember the ready plane.
Five oh four. A Coast Guard station in Pensacola hears: All planes in this flight join up in close formation.
Five oh seven. Change course to zero nine zero for ten minutes. East again.
Five oh nine. How long have we gone now? We are going too damn far north instead of east.
Remember the clocks? Nobody up there can say for sure how long they've been flying.
The same minute, the rescue station's log: the Lauderdale ready plane was held on the ground and would not go out.
Cox asks to take a plane out himself. As he told the Navy, the flight officer very definitely said no.
Five fifteen. We are now flying two seventy.
Five sixteen. We will fly two seventy until we hit the beach or run out of gas.
West, at last.
##
Five twenty-four. Pensacola again.
Planes fly close formation. When one plane drops to ten gallons of gas, all planes will land together.
Land together, on the sea.
Five minutes later, the sun sets at Fort Lauderdale. And tonight, there's almost no moon.
You may have read that ten gallons was their last message. It wasn't. It's in the log at five twenty-four. There's more than an hour and a half still to come.
Five thirty. Is that a ship on the left? The reply can't be read.
Again and again, the station asks Taylor to switch to the rescue frequency.
He won't. On a new channel, he could lose touch with his own four planes.
##
Now, what the Navy knew.
Since a quarter to five, stations along the coast have been taking bearings on Flight Nineteen's radio.
At five fifty, the lines cross. North of the Bahamas. East of Florida. Over open sea.
The Navy puts them within a hundred miles of one point, about two hundred thirty miles north of home.
Remember the wind? The Navy's review would later say it had apparently carried Taylor farther east than he realized.
About six ten, that position is phoned to the Fort Lauderdale tower. About six thirty-six, it goes out on the teletype.
No station ever broadcasts it to the five planes.
That's what the Navy knew by six. The men in the air never heard it.
##
Six oh four. Then this, typed at Port Everglades.
Holding course two seventy. Didn't go far enough east. Turn around again. We may just as well turn around and go east again.
Pensacola hears: Hello tower, this is Taylor. We are over the Gulf. We didn't go far enough east.
I suggest we fly due east until we run out of gas. We have a better chance of being picked up close to shore.
He still believes he's west of Florida, over the Gulf of Mexico. If he were, east would mean land.
He isn't. East of him is the open Atlantic.
An hour and a half earlier, someone in this flight said: two seventy. West.
At six oh six, the Board says, Taylor suggests to Captain Edward Powers, one of the students, that they turn east again.
After six oh seven, the courses they flew are unknown.
Six twenty-four. Port Everglades warns: This is serious. Their gas should be out by seven thirty.
Up the coast, at Banana River, two big Navy flying boats are being briefed for a night search. Thirteen men are about to take off in one of them.
##
Six forty-four. Through the static, the station can make out one call sign. F T three.
That's Ensign Joseph Bossee, twenty, the youngest pilot in the flight.
Seven oh four. The crash log at Fort Lauderdale: Last transmissions from the flight. Tee three calling Tee twenty-eight. No answer.
After that, the stations keep calling. F T twenty-eight, F T twenty-eight, how do you hear me, over. Until almost ten at night.
Nothing.
The Board's best guess: forced landings in darkness at sea, east of Florida, sometime after seven oh four. In a rough sea, with no moon.
Not a wing, not a raft, not one of the fourteen men was ever found.
##
Seven twenty-seven. A Navy flying boat takes off from Banana River. A P B M Mariner, Training Forty-Nine. Thirteen men.
Its pilot, Lieutenant junior grade Walter Jeffrey, volunteered for the night search.
Seven thirty. It radios that it's on its way. After that, nothing.
Seven fifty. Off New Smurna Beach, the tanker Gaines Mills sees a burst of flames. Apparently an explosion. Leaping flames a hundred feet high, burning ten minutes.
The escort carrier Solomons had the plane on its radar. At that exact time, in that exact spot, the blip disappeared and never came back.
The tanker steams through a big pool of oil and circles with searchlights. It finds no one.
You'll read that the rescue plane vanished without a trace, too. It didn't. Flames, oil, radar.
Twenty-three minutes after takeoff. The Board: exploded in the sea, for reason or reasons unknown.
Twenty-seven men, six planes, one night.
##
Thursday, at first light, the search begins.
By Friday, the Associated Press reports, the Navy has ordered two hundred forty-eight planes into the air, and eighteen ships, including the Solomons.
An object spotted in the water turns out to be a lard tub. Two men seen in a raft turn out to be a false report.
Monday, December tenth. The crash log: search secured.
The Navy was about to blame one man for all of it. His mother would fight back.
##
Within days, a Navy board of investigation meets in Miami.
It finds the rescue effort that evening ineffective. Radio interference. No switch to the rescue frequency. A broken teletype.
And it blames the leader: temporary mental confusion resulting in faulty judgment.
On February fifteenth, nineteen forty-six, an admiral goes further. He writes that Taylor became so hopelessly confused as to have suffered something akin to a mental aberration.
Taylor's mother, Katherine, a schoolteacher in Texas, never accepted it.
She posted a thousand-dollar reward, wrote more than two hundred letters, and took her son's case to a new Navy review board.
In nineteen forty-seven, the Navy changed the record. The cause: unknown.
So when you read that the Navy found Taylor guilty of a mental aberration: it was no trial, those weren't the Board's words, and his record was cleared.
And in nineteen ninety-one, someone announced they'd found the planes. We'll get there.
##
So where does the Bermuda Triangle come in? Not for years.
In nineteen fifty, an Associated Press writer lists Flight Nineteen among the sea's unsolved puzzles.
In nineteen sixty-two, a magazine story gives the men dramatic last words. We are entering white water. We're completely lost.
They're in no log and no testimony. Larry Kooshee, the writer who later traced the legend, called them fictionalized, dramatized messages.
The only white water in the record is Cox, at four o'clock, describing the sea below: covered with whitecaps and long white streamers.
In nineteen sixty-four, a writer in Argosy magazine coins the name: the Bermuda Triangle. Nineteen years after Flight Nineteen.
In nineteen seventy-four, a bestseller takes Don't come after me, then adds: they look like they are from outer space.
Three years later, a Hollywood film shows the five planes found in a desert, and their crews stepping off a spaceship.
The U S government doesn't recognize the Bermuda Triangle as an official name. And Noah says there's no evidence that disappearances happen there any more often than in other busy stretches of ocean.
##
May, nineteen ninety-one. A treasure-hunting ship called the Deep See finds five Avengers on the seabed, about ten miles off Fort Lauderdale. One shows the number twenty-eight.
The headlines say it: the Lost Patrol, found.
Less than three weeks later, the expedition takes it back. Quite certain that the five aircraft we found are not those of Flight Nineteen.
Wrong numbers. Older planes. More than a hundred Avengers were lost off Florida over the years.
And in twenty twenty-two, divers searching for Training Forty-Nine off Cape Canaveral found something else: a piece of the space shuttle Challenger.
##
So here's what the record can't tell us.
Where they are. The Navy's circle at five fifty was two hundred miles across. After six oh seven, nobody knows which way they flew.
When they went down, and whether together, as planned, or one by one.
Whether Taylor's compasses really failed. We only have his word.
And whether a broadcast of that position, or the ready plane, would have brought them home.
At seven oh four that night, one line went into the crash log. Tee three calling Tee twenty-eight. No answer.
The Navy has their words, their headings, their fuel, and a circle on a map. In eighty years, it has never found a single piece of them.
These are the fourteen men of Flight Nineteen, and the thirteen of Training Forty-Nine.
